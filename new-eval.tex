\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:experiments}

We evaluate whether \Fname preserves attack knowledge under a hard
communication budget while retaining predictive quality. We organize the
evaluation around five questions:
\begin{packeditemize}
    \item \textbf{RQ1: Detection quality under a budget.} How much predictive
    quality does the shared detector retain as communication decreases?
    \item \textbf{RQ2: Client label heterogeneity.} How do different client
    label distributions affect usable training partitions, class support, and
    detection quality?
    \item \textbf{RQ3: Selection signals and learners.} Which selection
    signals, semantic settings, and learning backends change the result?
    \item \textbf{RQ4: Score randomization.} How much detection quality
    remains as Laplace perturbation becomes stronger?
    \item \textbf{RQ5: Scalability and replay cost.} How do selection time and
    communication grow with the deployment?
\end{packeditemize}

\subsection{Experimental Setup}

\bheading{Datasets} We use four multiclass cybersecurity datasets:
CIC DDoS2019 (DDoS)~\cite{cicddos19}, CICMalDroid2020
(MalDroid)~\cite{maldroid}, CIC Darknet2020
(Darknet)~\cite{darknet}, and CIRA CIC DoHBrw 2020
(DoH)~\cite{DoH}. Each task contains 66 clients and 66 candidate detectors.
Table~\ref{tab:eval-datasets} summarizes the evaluation scale.

    class counts.}
    \label{tab:eval-datasets}
    \small

        \toprule
        Dataset & Training samples & Test samples & Features & Classes \\
        \midrule
        DDoS     & 3,355,966 & 1,127,526 & 79  & 14 \\
        MalDroid & 8,118     & 3,480     & 470 & 5  \\

        DoH      & 1,005,708 & 431,034   & 29  & 4  \\
        \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
\end{table*}

\bheading{Protocol} We divide each training pool into disjoint 60\%, 10\%,
and 30\% partitions for local detector training, selection validation, and
shared stacker training. We reserve the test set until both selection and
stacker fitting have finished. Unless stated otherwise, we use five repeated
splits, LightGBM~\cite{lightgbm} for local detectors and the shared stacker, a
50\% communication budget, minimum source support $n_{\min}=2$, class weights
that are inversely proportional to the square root of class frequency, and
equal weights for the three secondary selection signals.

\bheading{Comparison methods} We compare \Fname with three reference rules
under the same budget. The \emph{full pool} retains all 66 models and defines
the 100\% communication point. The \emph{random rule} samples a feasible set
and averages 20 fixed draws within each data split. The \emph{current utility
rule} ranks detectors by their individual validation quality. By contrast,
\Fname first maximizes weighted source class support and then refines current
predictive quality without reducing that support. We later remove only the
source support priority while retaining the same secondary search.

\bheading{Metrics and statistics} We choose macro F1 as the main detection
score because it gives every class equal weight. In imbalanced security
traffic, this prevents a frequent class from hiding weak detection of a rarer
attack class. We also report accuracy for overall correctness, balanced
accuracy for average class recall, and minimum shared class recall for the
weakest attack class that collaborating clients have in common. We measure
weighted class coverage separately because it describes retained training
support rather than classifier quality. For the DDoS privacy experiment, we
report \emph{attack leakage}, which is the fraction of predictions labeled
benign that are actually attacks. We count communication with the protocol
bytes defined in Eq.~\ref{eq:candidate_cost}.

We report means and 95\% Student \(t\) confidence intervals over five
repeated splits unless stated otherwise. When two conditions share a split,
we pair their observations. We apply Holm correction within each comparison
family fixed before analysis. We recompute every detection score from the
reserved test confusion matrix.

\bheading{Implementation} We run the experiments as a single machine
federation replay under Linux and Python~3.9.25. The software stack uses
NumPy~1.26.4, Scikit Learn~1.3.2, LightGBM~4.6.0, and
XGBoost~\cite{xgboost}~1.4.2. We record wall clock time and the logical bytes
exchanged by the evaluated protocol.

\subsection{RQ1: Detection Quality under a Budget}

first compare macro F1 at the central 50\% operating point. We then inspect
accuracy and minimum shared class recall at the same point. Finally, we trace
macro F1 across the full budget range.

At the 50\% target, \Fname uses from 49.5\% to 50.0\% of full pool
communication. It keeps macro F1 within 0.0052 of the full pool on MalDroid,
Darknet, and DoH. DDoS shows the larger gap of 0.0258. As
Fig.~\ref{fig:eval-budget-utility} shows, adding models does not always produce
a monotonic gain. MalDroid improves as the budget grows, whereas the other
datasets fluctuate within narrower ranges.

Across MalDroid, Darknet, and DoH, selecting half of the detectors changes
accuracy by at most 0.0046 and minimum shared class recall by at most 0.0061.
On DDoS, \Fname loses 0.0296 accuracy but only 0.0094 minimum shared class
recall. On Darknet, even the full pool reaches only 0.0077 recall on the
weakest shared class, compared with 0.0058 after selection. Both
configurations therefore reveal the same Darknet bottleneck.

DDoS also shows why the evaluation separates class support from detection
score. At the 25\% and 50\% budgets, the current utility rule obtains higher
macro F1 but retains only 0.774 and 0.910 of the attainable weighted support.
At both budgets, \Fname attains the full support value of 1.0. \Fname
therefore protects attainable attack class support while keeping the
detection score close to the full pool on three of the four tasks.

\subsection{RQ2: Client Label Heterogeneity}

When clients observe different attack classes, we evaluate both naturally
occurring and controlled label distributions. To preserve the original
deployment structure, we first keep the archived DDoS and Darknet client
assignments and repeat only the data split. To isolate label imbalance, we
then draw client partitions with Dirichlet parameters
$\alpha\in\{10,1,0.5,0.1\}$ and compare them with an independent and
identically distributed allocation. We require every accepted partition to
provide at least two local training classes to each client so that each local
detector remains a multiclass model.

\begin{figure*}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.98\textwidth]{figures/eval_heterogeneity.pdf}
    \caption{The figure shows how controlled client label imbalance changes
    feasibility and detection. Panel (a) counts usable partitions among ten
    attempts. Panel (b) measures label imbalance with Jensen Shannon
    divergence. Panels (c) and (d) report macro F1 at the 50\% budget. Error
    bars use the accepted partitions, with ten runs except for five MalDroid
    runs at $\alpha=1$.}
    \label{fig:eval-heterogeneity}
\end{figure*}

On Darknet, all ten partitions remain usable at every level, while measured
divergence rises from 0.0008 under the balanced allocation to 0.3516 at
$\alpha=0.1$. On MalDroid, all ten balanced and all ten $\alpha=10$
partitions remain usable, but only five $\alpha=1$ partitions pass the same
rule. None of the $\alpha=0.5$ or $0.1$ attempts can divide this smaller five
class training pool among 66 clients while retaining two classes per client.

Within the usable range, Darknet macro F1 stays between 0.5895 and 0.5976 at
the 50\% budget as imbalance grows. MalDroid stays between 0.8328 and 0.8375.
Under the original assignments, a larger budget helps the weakest class more
clearly. As the budget rises from 25\% to 75\%, Darknet macro F1 grows from
0.6162 to 0.6290 and minimum shared class recall grows from 0.0452 to 0.0964.
Over the same range, DDoS macro F1 changes from 0.6039 to 0.6104 and minimum
shared class recall changes from 0.0568 to 0.0773.

We then remove the source support priority while retaining the same secondary
search. Across 30 comparisons with the original assignments and 225
comparisons with controlled assignments, both versions choose the same set
and obtain the same macro F1, minimum class recall, communication, and
attained support. In these evaluated layouts, the secondary refinement
already reaches the maximum attainable support.

\subsection{RQ3: Selection Signals and Learners}

After the primary objective fixes maximum class support, \Fname uses current
evidence to order the remaining feasible sets. We remove individual detector
quality, classwise quality, and prediction diversity one at a time.
Because every variant still attains the maximum support, this comparison
isolates how the three signals order otherwise feasible detector sets.

At the 50\% budget, removing individual detector quality lowers DDoS macro F1
by 0.0190. Removing prediction diversity instead raises it by 0.0181 on the
same dataset. The direction changes across datasets and budgets. After Holm
correction, no removal produces a statistically significant change. The three
signals therefore work as a joint ranking rule rather than as independent
sources of universal improvement.

We also vary $n_{\min}\in\{1,2,5,10\}$ and compare class weights based on the
inverse square root of frequency with uniform weights. Across two fixed
Darknet layouts and two learner combinations, these choices leave the
selected set, support, macro F1, and minimum class recall unchanged. By
contrast, the learning backends produce visible score differences, as
Table~\ref{tab:eval-architecture} shows.

configurations with an XGBoost shared learner remain below the LightGBM
alternatives. The learning backend is therefore the clearest practical tuning
choice in this experiment.

\subsection{RQ4: Score Randomization}

To measure the utility cost of score randomization, we freeze the selected set
and add independent Laplace noise with scale $2/\epsilon$ to every score block
used for shared stacker training. A smaller $\epsilon$ adds stronger noise.
Here $\epsilon$ is the privacy parameter for one released score block. Under
the public label neighboring relation in
Theorem~\ref{thm:local_score_privacy}, two neighboring records share a label
and differ in one feature vector. The theorem gives label conditional record
level local differential privacy to each released block. When one record
contributes $s$ released blocks, sequential composition gives a total
parameter of $s\epsilon$ for that upload. During the centralized sensitivity
replay, we sample the same noise distribution after selection and keep the
test encoding fixed so that the curves isolate the effect on stacker training.

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.98\columnwidth]{figures/eval_privacy_utility.pdf}
    \caption{The figure shows how Laplace score randomization changes
    detection. Panel (a) reports macro F1. Panel (b) reports attacks hidden
    among predictions labeled benign. Larger $\epsilon$ adds less noise.
    Error bars show 95\% confidence intervals over the same five DDoS splits.}
    \label{fig:eval-privacy-utility}
\end{figure}

Without added noise, the selected half and the full pool obtain macro F1
scores of 0.6153 and 0.6412. At the strongest evaluated setting,
$\epsilon=1$, they obtain 0.5105 and 0.5298. The selected half loses 0.1048,
while the full pool loses 0.1114. Both configurations therefore retain a
similar fraction of their original detection score as perturbation grows.

When we inspect attack leakage, the curve reveals a sharper operational
change. For the selected half,
it rises from 0.055 without noise to 0.262 at $\epsilon=5$ and 0.583 at
$\epsilon=1$. For the full pool, it rises from 0.056 to 0.193 and then 0.644.
At the strongest perturbation, more than half of the records predicted as
benign are attacks. By reporting both macro F1 and attack leakage, the
evaluation shows how a moderate aggregate score can hide a costly security
failure.

\subsection{RQ5: Scalability and Replay Cost}

To verify exact selection directly, we compare \Fname with exhaustive search
at $C=8$ and $M\in\{8,10,12,16\}$. Across ten repetitions at every size, the
solver matches the exhaustive optimum in all 40 comparisons. Its median time
ranges from 37.70 to 118.72 ms, while every 95th percentile remains below
125 ms. We then vary the number of candidates and attack classes separately
in Fig.~\ref{fig:eval-solver-scaling}.

\begin{figure*}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.96\textwidth]{figures/eval_solver_scaling.pdf}
    \caption{The figure shows how selection time grows with the deployment.
    The left panel varies candidate models, while the right panel varies
    attack classes. Each panel uses an independently sampled sweep. Points
    show medians over ten repetitions, and upper bars mark the 95th
    percentile.}
    \label{fig:eval-solver-scaling}
\end{figure*}

With 12 attack classes, \Fname selects from 66 candidates in a median of
1.99 seconds and from 256 candidates in 33.73 seconds. With 66 candidates,
raising the attack vocabulary from 12 to 20 classes increases the median from
2.95 to 100.10 seconds. Within this range, attack class diversity drives time
more strongly than the number of candidate models.

experiment. The replay time includes every compared rule and budget within a
data split. The communication columns compare \Fname at the 50\% budget with
the full pool.

On MalDroid and Darknet, shared stacker training accounts for 74.9\% and
89.1\% of replay time. On DoH, shared training and selection comparisons use
33.2\% and 32.4\%. On DDoS, selection, shared training, and test evaluation
use 34.2\%, 31.7\%, and 28.2\%. The dominant computation therefore depends on
the data scale and learner rather than on selection alone.

At the 50\% budget, \Fname saves between 49.3\% and 50.4\% of total logical
communication. Score blocks form 93.6\% of full pool bytes on DDoS, whereas
model delivery forms 97.4\% on MalDroid, 93.4\% on Darknet, and 73.0\% on
DoH. The selected subset therefore reduces the transfer component that
dominates each task. At the main scale of 66 candidates, the exact selector
finishes in seconds and the complete protocol uses about half the logical
traffic of the full pool.
