\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:experiments}

We evaluate whether \Fname achieves its intended deployment trade-off:
preserve training-class support first, and then retain predictive quality
under a hard communication budget. Five questions organize the evaluation:
\begin{packeditemize}
    \item \textbf{RQ1: Detection quality under a budget.} How much
    Macro-F1 remains as the shared communication volume is reduced?
    \item \textbf{RQ2: Client heterogeneity.} How do fixed and controlled
    client label distributions affect usable training splits, class support,
    and detection quality?
    \item \textbf{RQ3: Design choices.} Which selection signals, semantic
    settings, and learning backends influence the result?
    \item \textbf{RQ4: Privacy--utility trade-off.} How much detection
    quality remains as Laplace perturbation becomes stronger?
    \item \textbf{RQ5: Scalability and cost.} How do selection time and
    communication grow with the deployment?
\end{packeditemize}

\subsection{Experimental Setup}

\bheading{Datasets} We use four multiclass cybersecurity datasets:
CIC-DDoS2019 (DDoS)~\cite{cicddos19}, CICMalDroid2020
(MalDroid)~\cite{maldroid}, CIC-Darknet2020
(Darknet)~\cite{darknet}, and CIRA-CIC-DoHBrw-2020
(DoH)~\cite{DoH}. Each task contains 66 clients and therefore 66 candidate
detectors. Table~\ref{tab:eval-datasets} summarizes the evaluation scale.

\begin{table*}[!t]
    \centering
    \caption{Datasets and evaluation scale.}
    \label{tab:eval-datasets}
    \small
    \begin{tabular}{lrrrr}
        \toprule
        Dataset & Training samples & Test samples & Features & Classes \\
        \midrule
        DDoS     & 3,355,966 & 1,127,526 & 79  & 14 \\
        MalDroid & 8,118     & 3,480     & 470 & 5  \\
        Darknet  & 99,066    & 42,459    & 76  & 11 \\
        DoH      & 1,005,708 & 431,034   & 29  & 4  \\
        \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
\end{table*}

\bheading{Protocol} We divide each training pool into disjoint 60\%, 10\%,
and 30\% partitions for local detector training, model-selection validation,
and shared-aggregator training. The held-out test set cannot influence model
eligibility, selection, or aggregator fitting. Unless stated otherwise, we
use five repeated splits, LightGBM~\cite{lightgbm} for both local detectors
and the global aggregator, a 50\% communication budget, minimum source
support $n_{\min}=2$, inverse-square-root class weights, and equal weights
for the three secondary selection signals.

\bheading{Comparison methods} The \emph{full pool} retains all 66 models and
defines the 100\% communication point. \emph{Random selection} samples a
feasible set and averages 20 fixed draws within each data split.
\emph{Utility-only ranking} favors detectors with high stand-alone validation
quality. \Fname first maximizes weighted training-class support
and then refines predictive quality without reducing that support. A matched
ablation later removes only the class-support priority while keeping the same
secondary search.

\bheading{Metrics and statistics} We use Macro-F1 as the main detection
score because it gives every class equal weight. This matters for imbalanced
security traffic: a frequent class cannot hide weak detection of a rarer
attack class. Accuracy measures overall correctness, balanced accuracy
averages recall across classes, and minimum shared-class recall exposes the
weakest class that collaborating clients have in common. Weighted class
coverage measures retained training support rather than classifier quality.
For the DDoS privacy experiment, we also report \emph{attack leakage}: the
fraction of predictions labeled benign that are actually attacks.
Communication is measured as the protocol bytes defined in
Eq.~\ref{eq:candidate_cost}.

Unless stated otherwise, points and table entries are means with 95\%
Student-$t$ confidence intervals over five repeated splits. Conditions are
paired on the same split, and pre-specified comparison families use Holm
correction. Detection scores are recomputed from the held-out confusion
matrices.

\bheading{Implementation} Experiments run as a single-machine federation
replay under Linux and Python~3.9.25, using NumPy~1.26.4,
scikit-learn~1.3.2, LightGBM~4.6.0, and
XGBoost~\cite{xgboost}~1.4.2. We measure wall-clock time and the exact logical
bytes exchanged by the evaluated protocol.

\subsection{RQ1: Detection Quality under Communication Budgets}

the signal needed by the shared detector. Table~\ref{tab:eval-main-results}
therefore compares Macro-F1 at the central 50\% budget.
Table~\ref{tab:eval-quality-retention} then tests whether this aggregate
score hides a loss in overall correctness or in the weakest class, while
Fig.~\ref{fig:eval-budget-utility} shows how Macro-F1 changes across the full
budget range.

At the 50\% target, \Fname retains 49.5--50.0\% of full-pool communication.
Its Macro-F1 is within 0.0052 of the full pool on MalDroid, Darknet, and DoH;
the DDoS gap is 0.0258. Figure~\ref{fig:eval-budget-utility} further shows
that more models do not guarantee a monotone gain: MalDroid improves as the
budget grows, whereas the other datasets fluctuate within comparatively
narrow ranges.

The broader metric view leads to the same conclusion. On MalDroid, Darknet,
and DoH, selecting half of the detectors changes accuracy by at most 0.0046
and weakest-class recall by at most 0.0061. DDoS has the larger accuracy
change (0.0296), but its weakest-class recall changes by only 0.0094. Darknet
also exposes a task-level bottleneck that Macro-F1 alone conceals: the
weakest shared class has recall 0.0077 with all detectors and 0.0058 after
selection. The near-zero value is therefore already present before the
communication budget is imposed.

DDoS reveals why class support and detection score must be measured
separately. At the 25\% and 50\% budgets, individual-score ranking obtains
higher Macro-F1 but retains only 0.774 and 0.910 of the attainable weighted
class support. \Fname attains 1.0 support at both budgets. Thus, ordered
selection converts a communication limit into an explicit class-support
guarantee while keeping the detection score close to the full pool on three
of the four tasks.

\subsection{RQ2: Robustness to Client Heterogeneity}

Budgeted collaboration matters most when clients observe different attack
classes. We study this in two complementary ways. The archived-layout study
(E3-A) keeps the original DDoS and Darknet client assignments and repeats the
train/validation split. The controlled study (E3-B) progressively
concentrates labels with Dirichlet parameters
$\alpha\in\{10,1,0.5,0.1\}$, alongside an IID setting. Every constructed
partition must still provide at least two local-training classes per client,
so the client detectors remain genuine multiclass models.

\begin{figure*}[!t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.98\textwidth]{figures/eval_heterogeneity.pdf}
    \caption{Detection under controlled client-label heterogeneity.
    (a) counts usable splits among ten attempts at each level; (b) measures
    the resulting client-label imbalance, where larger JSD means more uneven
    label mixtures; and (c--d) report Macro-F1 at three communication
    budgets. Confidence intervals in (c--d) use the usable splits ($n=10$,
    or $n=5$ for MalDroid at $\alpha=1$).}
    \label{fig:eval-heterogeneity}
\end{figure*}

The controlled construction reaches the full heterogeneity range on Darknet:
all ten partitions are valid at every level, and measured divergence rises
from 0.0008 under IID allocation to 0.3516 at $\alpha=0.1$. MalDroid
supports ten IID and ten $\alpha=10$ partitions, five $\alpha=1$
partitions, and none of the $\alpha=0.5$ or $0.1$ attempts meets the same
multiclass rule. This reveals a concrete data-regime boundary: a small
five-class training pool cannot always be divided among 66 clients while
preserving two local classes.

Within the valid range, detection remains stable. At the 50\% budget,
Darknet Macro-F1 spans 0.5895--0.5976 as divergence grows, and MalDroid spans
0.8328--0.8375. The fixed assignments show the same budget response: from
25\% to 75\%, Darknet Macro-F1 rises from 0.6162 to 0.6290 and its
weakest-class recall from 0.0452 to 0.0964; DDoS changes from 0.6039 to
0.6104 and from 0.0568 to 0.0773, respectively. Together, the two views
separate the effects: controlled partitions vary label imbalance, while fixed
assignments reveal how additional budget reaches the weakest class.

The fixed-layout and controlled experiments also provide a targeted ablation
of the class-support objective. Across 30 archived and 225
controlled matched comparisons, removing this priority changes neither the
selected set nor Macro-F1, minimum class recall, communication, or attained
class support. Increasing imbalance makes the coverage rule relevant, while
the quality-based refinement already reaches the same maximum in these
conditions. In these experiments, the ordered objective preserves attainable
class support while matching the refinement's selected sets, detection
quality, and communication.

\subsection{RQ3: Which Design Choices Influence Detection?}

Once maximum class support is fixed, secondary signals decide among the
remaining feasible model sets. Figure~\ref{fig:eval-component-ablation}
removes individual detector quality, class-aware quality, and prediction
diversity one at a time. Showing the absolute Macro-F1 keeps the comparison
on the same detection scale as the main result.

At the 50\% budget, the paired Macro-F1 changes range from $-0.0190$ when
individual quality is removed on DDoS to $+0.0181$ when prediction diversity
is removed on the same dataset. The signs change across datasets and
budgets, and no removal remains significant after Holm correction. The
secondary signals therefore act jointly; none supplies a consistent
stand-alone improvement across the evaluated tasks.

\begin{figure}[!t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.98\columnwidth]{figures/eval_component_ablation.pdf}
    \caption{Macro-F1 for the full design and one-at-a-time removals of
    detector quality, class-aware quality, and prediction diversity. Error
    bars show 95\% confidence intervals over five splits.}
    \label{fig:eval-component-ablation}
\end{figure}

We also vary $n_{\min}\in\{1,2,5,10\}$ and compare inverse-square-root
with uniform class weights on two fixed Darknet layouts and two learner
combinations. The selected set, class coverage, Macro-F1, and minimum class
recall remain unchanged throughout this fixed grid. In contrast, changing
the local and global learning backends produces visible score differences,

The highest means use LightGBM locally with XGBoost as the shared learner on

other Macro-F1 comparisons do not separate after correction. Learner choice
is therefore the clearer practical tuning lever, while the tested semantic
grid and individual secondary signals show no universal winner.

\subsection{RQ4: Privacy--Utility Trade-off}

Local score randomization is useful only if the shared detector retains
enough predictive signal. We freeze the selected set and add independent
Laplace noise with scale $2/\epsilon$ to each score block used to train the
global aggregator. Smaller $\epsilon$ means stronger perturbation. This is
the same release rule analyzed by
Theorem~\ref{thm:local_score_privacy}; evaluation uses a common test encoding
so that the curve isolates the effect on aggregator learning.

\begin{figure}[!t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.98\columnwidth]{figures/eval_privacy_utility.pdf}
    \caption{Privacy--utility trade-off under Laplace score randomization.
    Panel (a) reports aggregate detection quality; panel (b) reports attacks
    hidden among predictions labeled benign. Larger $\epsilon$ means less
    noise; error bars show 95\% confidence intervals over the same five DDoS
    splits.}
    \label{fig:eval-privacy-utility}
\end{figure}

Without added noise, the selected-half and full-pool configurations obtain
0.6153 and 0.6412 Macro-F1. At the strongest evaluated setting
($\epsilon=1$), they obtain 0.5105 and 0.5298, respectively. Intermediate
points fluctuate, but the broad response is clear: stronger perturbation
reduces the detection signal available to the global learner. The total
drop is 0.1048 for the selected half and 0.1114 for the full pool.

The security-oriented metric reveals a sharper transition. For the selected
half, attack leakage rises from 0.055 without noise to 0.262 at
$\epsilon=5$ and 0.583 at $\epsilon=1$; the full pool changes from 0.056 to
0.193 and 0.644. Thus, the strongest perturbation makes more than half of the
predictions labeled benign correspond to attacks, even though Macro-F1
remains above 0.5. Reporting both panels prevents the aggregate score from
hiding this operational cost. The selected half loses 0.0066 less Macro-F1
than the full pool over the same noise range, combining lower communication
with a comparable privacy--utility response. Together, the theorem and these
measurements connect the score-upload privacy parameter to its detection cost.

\subsection{RQ5: Scalability and End-to-End Cost}

Exact selection is useful only if it remains fast as deployments add models
or attack classes. We first compare \Fname with exhaustive search at
$C=8$ and $M\in\{8,10,12,16\}$. All ten repeats at each size match the
exhaustive optimum, for 40 of 40 agreements; median time ranges from 37.70 to
118.72 ms and every 95th percentile remains below 125 ms. This direct comparison verifies exactness independently of the larger
scaling experiment. We then vary candidates and classes in Fig.~\ref{fig:eval-solver-scaling}.

\begin{figure}[!t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.98\columnwidth]{figures/eval_solver_scaling.pdf}
    \caption{Time required to select models as the deployment grows. The
    panels independently vary candidate models and attack classes on a
    logarithmic time scale. Points are medians over ten repeats; upper bars
    mark the 95th percentile.}
    \label{fig:eval-solver-scaling}
\end{figure}

With 12 attack classes, selecting from 66 candidate models takes a median of
1.99 seconds; increasing the pool to 256 models raises this to 33.73 seconds.
With 66 candidates, increasing the attack vocabulary from 12 to 20 classes
raises the median from 2.95 to 100.10 seconds. Target-class diversity is
therefore the stronger driver of selection time in this range.

Table~\ref{tab:eval-system-cost} connects this isolated selection time to the
full experiment. The total replay time includes every compared selection rule
and budget within a data split, whereas communication compares \Fname at the
50\% budget with all 66 models.

Shared-model training accounts for 74.9\% of MalDroid time and 89.1\% of
Darknet time. DoH divides time almost evenly between shared-model training
(33.2\%) and selection comparisons (32.4\%), while DDoS spreads most of its
cost across selection (34.2\%), shared training (31.7\%), and test evaluation
(28.2\%). The system bottleneck therefore depends on the data scale and model
backend rather than on selection alone.

Figure~\ref{fig:eval-communication-breakdown} explains why selecting half of
the models consistently saves 49.3--50.4\% of total logical communication.
DDoS is score-upload dominated: probability blocks form 93.6\% of its
full-pool bytes. Model delivery instead forms 97.4\% on MalDroid, 93.4\% on
Darknet, and 73.0\% on DoH. The selector reduces the model-dependent term
that dominates each task. Together, the timing and communication results
show that deployment-sized selection finishes in seconds at the main
66-model scale and approximately halves protocol traffic.
