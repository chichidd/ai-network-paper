\title{\red{\Fname: Reusing Frozen Intrusion Detectors with Site-Specific Adoption Audits}}

% Anonymous submission: author names, affiliations, and email addresses are omitted.
\author{}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}

\red{In practice, organizations may retain deployed intrusion detectors after their historical training material becomes unavailable. Existing federated learning methods instead assume that sites collaborate while training or updating their base detectors. We study post-training federation, where sites reuse frozen detector packages with limited current labels while keeping per-example current site data local. \Fname separates collaborative candidate construction from site-local deployment authorization. For construction, the sites learn an incumbent-anchored residual composer from securely aggregated gradients and personalize it with local fitting data. Under non-collusion, the coordinator receives no individual composer gradient in plaintext. For authorization, each site freezes its candidate before opening held-out audit data and compares it with a fixed incumbent comparator. We show that unlabeled observations alone do not generally identify even which predictor has lower current classification error. We then give a finite-sample guarantee relative to the audited incumbent and characterize audit power when the population excess risk lies below the adoption tolerance by a positive margin. Together, these results support site-specific deployment decisions under an incumbent-relative, distribution-specific guarantee.}

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

\red{In operational networks, operators often keep an intrusion detector in service long after its original training run. However, they cannot always keep the historical training material needed to reproduce or update it. For example, storage limits can cause old logs to be overwritten~\cite{acsc2024logging,microsoft2026retention}. At the same time, traffic changes after deployment. Under such drift, a detector that once worked well may become stale~\cite{nist2023airmf}. Thus, an operator may still have a usable detector package after the material behind it has disappeared.}

\red{When historical training material remains available, an operator can retrain its detector. However, we study sites that no longer retain this material. We assume that each detector remains available as a frozen package with a common model interface. At the same time, each site can label a limited current sample and query the packages locally. We call the resulting local observations \emph{current site data}. In this setting, the operator aims to reuse the frozen packages without sending per-example current site data. We call this setting \emph{post-training federation}. Here, post-training means that all base detectors are frozen before collaboration; the sites train only a composer over their outputs.}

\red{Although the historical training material is gone, the packages can still process current records. Moreover, their responses may complement one another even when no package is reliable by itself. Thus, the problem is not to rebuild the detectors. Instead, we must decide whether combining the packages' current responses can improve overall performance while keeping each accepted candidate within a chosen loss tolerance of its audited incumbent.}

\red{Prior model-reuse methods either operate outside a federation or exchange per-example information~\cite{old_dogs2019,ma2023consensus}. Federated methods instead train or update their base models during collaboration~\cite{qiu2024fedmstacking,ditto2021}, while model-comparison methods assume that the challenger has already been constructed~\cite{sawade2012activecomparison}. These lines of work do not jointly address frozen-before-collaboration reuse, example-local collaboration, and incumbent-relative adoption at each site. Section~\ref{sec:related} examines this gap.}

\red{Against this background, we ask: how can several sites reuse frozen intrusion detectors after training while keeping current site data local and limiting site-specific excess risk relative to each audited incumbent? The central difficulty is that candidate fitting and deployment authorization use different data. When sites share useful correction structure, collaborative fitting can pool limited signals. Yet an average gain cannot justify replacing one site's incumbent, and unlabeled outputs alone do not generally reveal which predictor has lower current classification error. Moreover, the collaboration must not expose an individual site gradient in plaintext. A useful design must therefore learn from shared structure while leaving deployment authorization to held-out local audit data.}

\red{We address this tension by separating candidate construction from deployment authorization. \Fname represents a candidate as a residual correction around the incumbent, so the composer has an exact no-change state. The sites estimate a common correction using an existing secure-aggregation primitive, which enforces the collaboration boundary without exposing per-example current site data~\cite{bonawitz2017secureaggregation}. Because package utility differs across sites, each site adapts the shared result rather than treating it as a final detector.}

\red{Local adaptation produces only a candidate. Before using held-out audit data, the site freezes this candidate and its audit rule. The audit then compares the candidate with a fixed transformation of the incumbent and alone authorizes deployment. We show why label-free observations cannot generally make this comparison, bound the adopted predictor's excess risk relative to the audited incumbent, and characterize when an acceptable candidate is likely to pass.}

\red{We make three contributions.}
\begin{packeditemize}
    \item \red{We define post-training federation under an explicit trust boundary and identify the need to separate collaborative candidate construction from site-local authorization. We show that unlabeled outputs alone do not generally reveal whether a frozen candidate has lower current classification error than its incumbent.}
    \item \red{We construct candidates with an incumbent-anchored composer that moves from shared fitting to local personalization. Under non-collusion, an existing secure-aggregation primitive keeps individual plaintext gradients from the coordinator.}
    \item \red{We authorize deployment with a paired local audit. We give a simultaneous finite-sample guarantee relative to the audited incumbents and characterize when a candidate below the adoption tolerance is likely to pass.}
\end{packeditemize}

\subsection{Experimentation Setup}

The experiments are conducted on a machine installed Ubuntu 20.04 LTS with one CPU AMD Ryzen Threadripper 3960X and 96GB memory. 
We use a client--server FL simulation environment comprising a client module and a server module, and the training process follows FedAvg~\cite{federated_learning}.

\subsubsection{Datasets}
We conduct experiments on the following datasets: a partition sampled from CIC-DDoS2019\cite{cicddos19} (DDoS2019), CICMalDroid2020\cite{maldroid} (MalDroid2020), CIC-Darknet2020\cite{darknet} (Darknet2020), and CIRA-CIC-DoHBrw-2020\cite{DoH} (DoHBrw2020). 
The datasets DDoS2019, Darknet2020, and DoHBrw-2020 contain traffic packet statistic data, \eg flow rate and mean of packet size \etc of network for DDoS attack classification, darknet traffic, and DNS-over-HTTPS (DoH) traffic. 
The Maldroid2020 is an Android malware classification dataset containing static and dynamic analysis patterns for 11,598 Android samples.

Then, in order to prove that \Fname is able to detect the unknown attack as well, we keep the attack ``PORTMAP'' only in the test dataset which means such an attack is not included in the NIDS training process.

\subsubsection{Baseline model reproduction}
We choose FedAvg~\cite{federated_learning} with MLP models of $3$, $5$, and $7$ layers as our baseline models. Each hidden layer of MLP has $64$ neurons, and the output layer is equal to the number of classes. We also compare our results with centralized training approaches including XGBoost\cite{xgboost} (noted as X) and 
LightGBM\cite{lightgbm} (noted as L) which can be seen as the best performance achieved by GBDT if we gather client data to the server.

In our implementation, since MLP models are sensitive to the scale of data as well as infinity (Inf) and missing values (NaN), we first replace invalid values with $0$, then we preprocess the rest data in order to stabilize the training:

\begin{packeditemize}
    \item Logarithm operation. To reduce the order of magnitude of training data $\mathbf{X}$, for $i$-th column $\mathbf{X}[i]$, $\mathbf{X}[i]\leftarrow \log(\mathbf{X}[i]-\min(\mathbf{X}[i])+1)$, where $\min(\mathbf{X}[i])$ is minimum value of $\mathbf{X}[i]$.
    \item Standardization. For $i$-th column $\mathbf{X}[i]$, $\mathbf{X}[i]\leftarrow \frac{\mathbf{X}[i] - mean(\mathbf{X}[i])}{std(\mathbf{X}[i])}$, where $mean$ and $std$ stand for mean and standard deviation value of the column, respectively. Note that this preprocessing operation does not apply to binary feature.
\end{packeditemize} 

To evaluate the performance of MLP, it is necessary to apply the same preprocessing for test data using the min, mean, and standard deviation values that are calculated for preprocessing training data. 
Remark that computing min, mean, and standard deviation of each column is achievable under FL setting: it only needs to compute the sum, the squared sum as well as the number of samples of client data to compute a global value of mean and standard deviation.

We train each MLP model to achieve the highest accuracy score by gird search of hyperparameters: learning rate $lr\in\{0.1, 0.01\}$, maximum round of training for 1,000, 1,500 and 2,000, client batch size of each round 64, 128, and 256. 
We test SGD and Adam\cite{adam} as optimizers.

We use raw data for GBDT models we used for experiments, \ie XGBoost and LightGBM, as they are not sensitive to the order of magnitude and data noise. 
The most influential hyperparameters are been optimized through grid search: $n\_estimators$ ranges from 30 to 200 with step 10, $max\_depth$ from 4 to 10. As for LightGBM, $num\_leaves$ needs to be adjusted according to $max\_depth$, we set $num\_leaves = \lfloor \frac{2}{3}2^{max\_depth} \rfloor$.

\subsubsection{Metrics}
To evaluate the classification performance, we measure and compare the accuracy score, \ie the ratio of correctly classified samples in the test dataset. 
Another important function of NIDS is the attack detection rate, \ie the ability to precisely distinguish malicious and benign data. 
Here, we use the miss rate, \ie the ratio of attack data that are falsely classified as benign over all the benign predictions. 
We also use the F1 score to evaluate the attack detection performance, as it pays equal attention to the precision and recalls in binary classification tasks.

% \noindent{\textbf{Communication Overhead.}}
We analyze the communication cost required by \acp{nn}-based NIDS and \Fname. 
To quantify communication cost, we measure the transmission data size, \ie the data sent between server and clients during training. 

For the NN-based NIDS model trained by FedAvg, the server sends model parameters to each client and received updated parameters from clients each round. 
Therefore, the communication cost for uploading and downloading are equal, and the complexity is $O(KNR)$, where $R$ is the number of training rounds, and $K$ is the model size and $N$ is the number of participants. 
In our experiment, we set $R=1000$ and $N=66$. 
The model size refers to the weight and bias parameters of MLP, and $K$ equals $14286$ and $30926$ respectively for 3-layer and 7-layer MLPs ($79$ neurons for input layer, $14$ neurons for output layer, and $64$ neurons per hidden layer). 

For \Fname, the uploading and downloading costs are different for the training is different from FedAvg. Specifically, each client uploads trained encoder and encoding data, which takes $O(KN + B_t\times (c_M M +1))$, where $K$ is encoder size, $B_t$ is predefined training data budget, and $c_M$ the average number of classes of encoder among $M$ selected encoders. 
The last $1$ signifies label data. 
The clients download $M$ selected encoders, which demands $O(KNM)$. 
In our experiment, we take all the client data and all the encoders, thus $B_t=3355966$, $M=N=66$ and $c_M M =156$. 
The largest encoder contains $3\times 60$ trees (3 classes and $n\_estimators$ 60) and each tree contains $10$ leaves, thus the encoder size $K$ is smaller than $10 \times 60 \times 3 = 1800$.

We summarize complexity and parameter number for uploading and downloading in \tablename~\ref{tab:communication_overhead}, and transmission data size when each parameter takes $8$ bytes in memory. We observe that FedAvg takes a larger flow quantity than \Fname. More importantly, the downloading data size is much bigger than that of \Fname, which further hinders training speed, since the downloading is generally slower than uploading and the clients need more time to download parameters. 
As for \Fname, the clients only need to download selected encoders for accelerating the total training process.

\subsubsection{Accuracy score (ACC) of different data masking}

As mentioned in Section~\ref{sec:problem}, the clients generate random masking on the feature and label data before the training starts. 
Another benefit is that the clients do not need to transmit the masked data to the server and further reduce communication overhead. 
However, the masked data may contain critical features and decrease the model's performance. 
Particularly, the masking on the feature data is more worth studying since it covers the majority of the data, \ie feature data. 
Thus, we investigate the impact of feature data masking on the model.

\subsection{Machine Unlearning}

The right to be forgotten attracts increasing attention and is protected by legislations such as the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR)~\cite{gdpr} in Europe. 
Bourtoule~\etal have introduced machine unlearning \cite{unlearning} which aims to erase the effect of data to be unlearned from a trained model. 
They have also proposed SISA, a training framework that allows data to be unlearned from the model with a minimal cost. 
Specifically, SISA partitions the training dataset into small parts called shards, and train one constituent model for each shard. 
In this way, when some data need to be unlearned, only the model of the shard containing the data has to be retrained from the last checkpoint where the data is not introduced into the training process. 

For the machine unlearning, \Fname is designed to be similar to the SISA by regarding client data as the shards. 
If some client wants to be unlearned, it only needs to delete the corresponding encoding data and retrain the server classifier. 
For the client who wants to be unlearned but whose encoder has been spread to other clients during training, the server has to select another available encoder to encode client data. 
Nevertheless, the major cost is still retraining the server classifier. Consequently, 
\Fname allows participants to freely unlearn data without exerting much influence on the server.

\section{\red{Discussion and Limitations}}\label{sec:discuss}

\red{Our analysis explains why the deployment decision uses held-out current labels. Without an assumption that links labels to the observed outputs, the same label-free view can correspond to opposite classification-error rankings of the candidate and incumbent. Thus, the audit supplies information that output-only comparison lacks.}

\red{Under the block assumption, a valid audit controls deployed excess risk relative to the audited incumbent across all preselected site--metric comparisons. The total failure budget bounds the probability that this joint statement fails. The power result gives the complementary conclusion: when every population excess risk at a site lies below its adoption tolerance by the required margin, $\beta_j$ bounds the probability that the site does not accept the candidate. A smaller audit may therefore retain an acceptable candidate.}

\red{The adoption analysis does not depend on how the frozen candidate was trained. In particular, it does not show that shared training outperforms local-only fitting.}

\red{This result remains relative to the audited incumbent and the distribution covered by the audit. A systematic label error changes the risk being controlled, so the site must correct its labeling process before another audit. Likewise, a post-audit shift ends the prior guarantee and requires an audit on the new distribution. Moreover, the result does not establish that the incumbent itself is safe.}

\red{Separately, our collaboration boundary follows Section~\ref{sec:threat_model}. Under non-collusion, secure aggregation prevents the coordinator from receiving an individual composer gradient in plaintext. However, it provides neither differential privacy nor protection against inference from the shared training state.}

\red{The statistical guarantee assumes that both predictors execute as audited. It does not cover the runtime response in Section~\ref{sec:approach} when this condition fails.}

\red{Prediction cost grows with the package pool. We leave cost-aware package selection to future work.}

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
\red{We introduce \Fname for post-training federation, where sites reuse frozen intrusion detectors after their historical training material becomes unavailable. \Fname learns an incumbent-anchored composer through secure aggregation, personalizes it locally, and lets each site decide adoption with held-out data. We show that unlabeled outputs alone cannot generally rank the candidate and incumbent by current classification error. Under the audit assumptions, we then bound deployed excess risk jointly across the selected comparisons and give a sufficient condition for a candidate below the adoption tolerance to pass. These results remain relative to the audited incumbent and the covered distribution; they do not establish absolute detector safety.}
