\begin{abstract}
Effective intrusion detection benefits from diverse attack data, yet raw historical source records may no longer be accessible due to privacy and retention constraints, preventing further collaborative training.
In this paper, we study selective integration of authorized pretrained detectors when raw source records are unavailable but coarse source class counts remain available for reuse.
Under a hard deployment budget, the goal is to choose a fixed detector set that preserves diverse source class coverage while maintaining predictive quality in the deployment domain.
We present \Fname, which gives weighted source class coverage strict priority over deployment-domain predictive behavior rather than combining them into a single score.
\Fname aligns heterogeneous detector outputs and uses held-out deployment-domain calibration records to assess deployment-domain predictive behavior.
It then exactly maximizes weighted source class coverage under a logical byte budget via dynamic programming, refines the selected set without reducing coverage, and fits a shared stacker.
To limit privacy leakage, we also protect uploaded score blocks via local differential privacy.
Theoretically, we prove that the calibration bounds remain valid after adaptive selection, that the primary dynamic program is exact, and that perturbed scores satisfy label-conditional local differential privacy.
Across four intrusion detection datasets with more than 60 candidate detectors per task, \Fname reduces modeled logical communication by approximately half under a 50\% selection-cost budget while preserving maximum weighted source class coverage and near-full-pool detection quality.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Effective intrusion detection relies on training data that capture diverse traffic conditions and attack behaviors.
However, data collected by a single organization typically reflect only its own operational environment, limiting the diversity of attack patterns available for training.
Collaborative learning, particularly Federated Learning (FL), can mitigate such data silos by learning from distributed records without pooling them centrally~\cite{federated_learning,Fedprox,ditto2021,zhao2023fedprompt}.
Yet these approaches still require contributors to retain local training records and participate in additional model optimization.
In practice, raw historical network logs may become inaccessible because they can contain sensitive operational and personally identifiable information~\cite{nist2016threatsharing,xu2026entente}, and may need to be deleted under privacy and retention requirements such as the GDPR~\cite{gdpr2018general}.

When these raw records are unavailable but authorized pretrained detectors and coarse source class counts remain, reusing the detectors offers a way to exploit complementary source knowledge without revisiting the original data~\cite{old_dogs2019}.
However, existing approaches either select or aggregate pretrained models without explicitly accounting for heterogeneous deployment costs~\cite{dong2022zood,zhao2024modelglue}, or perform cost-aware detector selection rather than choosing one fixed detector set for deployment~\cite{birman2022spirel}.
This gap motivates our research question: \emph{How can a coordinator selectively integrate a fixed set of pretrained intrusion detectors under a deployment budget while preserving source class coverage and maintaining detection performance in the deployment domain?}

Answering this question presents three challenges.
First, detectors contributed by different parties may use different label spaces and support different target classes, making their outputs difficult to compare and integrate directly.
Second, source class coverage and deployment-domain detection performance may favor different detectors: a detector with unique class coverage may no longer perform well after distribution shifts, whereas a detector that performs well in the deployment domain may add little new coverage.
The coordinator should therefore preserve unique source coverage without filling the limited budget with detectors that are weak or redundant in the deployment domain.
Third, detector selection must satisfy the deployment budget while remaining statistically reliable and privacy-aware.
Because calibration data are used to choose detectors, their performance estimates must remain valid after selection, while uploaded prediction scores may still reveal sensitive information.

To address these challenges, we present \Fname, a framework for selective integration of pretrained intrusion detectors under deployment budgets.
The key idea is to prioritize source class coverage over deployment-domain predictive performance: \Fname first maximizes weighted source class coverage under the deployment budget and then improves deployment-domain predictive behavior without reducing that coverage.
The two objectives use different information: coarse source class counts indicate which target classes were represented during training, whereas held-out deployment-domain calibration records indicate how well the pretrained detectors perform after distribution shift~\cite{yang2021cade}.
\Fname first aligns heterogeneous detector outputs to a common label space and uses the calibration records to assess their deployment-domain predictive behavior.
It then solves the primary coverage problem through dynamic programming, refines the selected set without sacrificing coverage, and integrates the selected outputs with a shared stacker~\cite{wolpert1992stacked}.

We prove that the calibration bounds remain valid after adaptive detector selection and that the primary dynamic program is exact.
Because avoiding raw-data sharing alone does not eliminate privacy risks from model outputs~\cite{ico2023aiguidance}, \Fname also supports optional Laplace perturbation before score upload.
The perturbed scores satisfy record-level, label-conditional local differential privacy; this guarantee applies to the uploaded scores, not to public labels or contributed detector parameters.

We evaluate \Fname on four intrusion detection datasets with over 60 candidate detectors per task.
At a 50\% selection-cost budget, \Fname maximizes weighted source class coverage while reducing modeled logical communication by 49.3\% to 50.4\%, demonstrating that diverse source coverage can be retained at roughly half the communication cost.
Importantly, these savings incur only a small loss in detection quality: across all four datasets, the macro F1 gap from the full detector pool is limited to 0.028.
In all 40 enumerable comparison instances, the primary solver attains the same coverage as exhaustive search.
In summary, we make four contributions.

\begin{packeditemize}
\item We formulate budget-constrained selective integration of pretrained intrusion detectors when raw historical source records are unavailable and introduce an ordered objective that prioritizes source class coverage over deployment-domain predictive behavior.

\item We develop \Fname to align heterogeneous detectors, exactly maximize weighted source class coverage under the budget, refine the selected set without reducing coverage, and integrate their outputs with a shared stacker.

\item We prove that the calibration bounds remain valid after adaptive detector selection, that the primary coverage problem is solved exactly, and that local refinement preserves this optimum; we also establish label-conditional local differential privacy for randomized score uploads.

\item We evaluate \Fname on four intrusion detection tasks, showing that a 50\% selection-cost budget roughly halves modeled logical communication while preserving maximum source class coverage and near-full-pool detection quality.
\end{packeditemize}

\section{Background and Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
We first review existing collaborative training and model integration methods, and then introduce the notion of differential privacy.

\bheading{Retraining and Adaptation of Intrusion Detectors}
When participants retain local training data and can rerun optimization, federated learning coordinates their parameter updates without transferring raw data~\cite{federated_learning,Fedprox,ditto2021}.
Within intrusion detection, federated methods apply collaborative training across distributed network environments~\cite{pourahmadi2023fedoe,xu2026entente}.
Recent NIDS research also redesigns the training pipeline to withstand adverse data conditions.
For example, RAPIER handles low-quality labels for encrypted malicious traffic~\cite{qing2024rapier}, Rosetta uses TCP-aware augmentation to improve robustness across network environments~\cite{xie2023rosetta}, and CoLD denoises labels before fitting a classifier~\cite{yang2026cold}.
These approaches improve training or adaptation when the underlying records remain available to the learning procedure.
In contrast, we consider the setting where the raw historical records are unavailable, while the pretrained detectors and coarse class-count metadata remain authorized for reuse.

\bheading{Pretrained Model Integration}
When source training data are no longer available but their trained models remain accessible, pretrained model integration is the closest line of work.
ZooD ranks a heterogeneous model zoo by cross-domain transferability and aggregates its top $K$ models~\cite{dong2022zood}, while Model-GLUE studies selection and aggregation strategies for heterogeneous pretrained model zoos~\cite{zhao2024modelglue}.
However, these methods focus on transfer or integration utility rather than the hard aggregate selection-cost constraint studied here.

\bheading{Output-Level Ensembles and Cost-Aware Selection}
Once detector outputs are available, stacked generalization can train a
downstream classifier over them~\cite{wolpert1992stacked}.
SIRAJ aggregates outputs from heterogeneous threat-intelligence sources using a pretrain-and-fine-tune framework~\cite{thirumuruganathan2022siraj}, while FedMStacking uses stacking to support heterogeneous local models with misaligned labels~\cite{qiu2024fedmstacking}.
To account for inference cost, SPIREL learns a per-item policy that trades off model invocations against classification utility~\cite{birman2022spirel}.
These approaches cover complementary pieces of our setting: output ensembles combine available predictions, while SPIREL controls cost through per-item invocation decisions.
\Fname instead makes a one-time subset decision after deployment-domain screening under a hard aggregate budget and then fits a shared classifier over the selected outputs.

Table~\ref{tab:related_comparison} summarizes the combination of properties most relevant to our deployment setting: deployment-domain screening, fixed subset selection under a hard aggregate budget, source-class priority, and shared stacking over only the selected outputs. This combination motivates the ordered selection problem formalized next.

\bheading{Local Differential Privacy}
Prediction outputs can reveal information about their inputs, so a privacy
guarantee must specify both where randomization occurs and which fields it
protects.
%
For example, PrivateKT perturbs locally produced predictions before uploading
them for knowledge transfer~\cite{qi2023privatekt}, whereas differentially
private federated trees protect the statistics exchanged while training a new
model~\cite{dpfedtree_ccs22}.
%
Our setting differs because \Fname uploads scores from selected detectors to
fit a shared classifier while treating labels as public.
%
We therefore specialize the local model of Duchi et
al.~\cite{duchi2013localprivacy} to this release.

{
\begin{definition}[Label-conditional local differential privacy]
\label{def:local_dp}
For a fixed public label $y$, a contributor applies a randomized mechanism
$\mathcal M_y$ to the feature vector $x$.
%
The upload
$\mathcal R(x,y)=(\mathcal M_y(x),y)$ satisfies label-conditional
$(\epsilon,0)$-local differential privacy if, for every fixed $y$, every pair
$x,x'$, and every measurable output set $\mathcal O$,
\begin{equation}
\Pr[\mathcal M_y(x)\in\mathcal O]
\le e^{\epsilon}\Pr[\mathcal M_y(x')\in\mathcal O].
\label{eq:local_dp_definition}
\end{equation}

This definition compares records with the same public label and protects the
feature-derived portion of the upload, and the label itself remains public.
\end{definition}
}

To instantiate Definition~\ref{def:local_dp}, let a deterministic score map
$\psi$ have replacement sensitivity
\begin{equation}
\Delta_1(\psi)=\sup_{x,x'}\lVert \psi(x)-\psi(x')\rVert_1.
\label{eq:local_l1_sensitivity}
\end{equation}

Adding independent noise
$\eta_k\sim\operatorname{Lap}(0,\Delta_1(\psi)/\epsilon)$ to every coordinate
yields a mechanism that satisfies
Definition~\ref{def:local_dp}~\cite{dwork2006calibrating}.
If one record releases detector score vectors with parameters
$\epsilon_1,\ldots,\epsilon_s$, sequential composition gives the parameter
$\sum_{i=1}^{s}\epsilon_i$.
Under one record replacement, releases derived from unchanged records do not
increase this privacy parameter.
In \Fname, this mechanism applies to the selected detector score vectors before they are assembled into uploaded score blocks.
Theorem~\ref{thm:local_score_privacy} establishes label-conditional local
privacy for the resulting score upload under the public label adjacency
relation.

\section{Problem Statement}
\label{sec:problem}


In this section, we formalize the problem of selective integration of pretrained intrusion detectors when the raw historical source records used to train them are unavailable.
We first define the information available for detector reuse, map heterogeneous detector outputs to a common deployment label space, and model the communication cost of deploying a selected subset.
We then specify the threat model for score uploads and formulate a budget-constrained selection objective that prioritizes source class coverage before deployment-domain predictive behavior.

\subsection{System Model}

We consider $J$ contributors that provide a pool of $M$ pretrained intrusion detectors for integration.
The raw records originally used to fit these detectors are no longer available to the integration process, while the metadata needed to interpret and deploy the detectors remain available.
For candidate $i$, let $f_i$ denote the pretrained detector, with native probability output $p_i(x)\in\mathbb R_{\ge0}^{h_i}$ satisfying $\mathbf 1^\top p_i(x)=1$, serialized model size $K_i$ bytes, and coarse source class-count vector $H_i=(H_{i,1},\ldots,H_{i,C})$ after semantic mapping, where $H_{i,c}$ records the source training count mapped to target class $c$.
Because contributors may use different class definitions, we also associate each candidate with a semantic map that specifies how its native outputs correspond to the deployment label space.

We first make these heterogeneous outputs comparable.
Let the deployment label space contain $C$ target classes.
For candidate $i$, the coordinator uses a semantic map
\begin{equation}
A_i\in\{0,1\}^{(C+1)\times h_i},
\qquad
\mathbf 1_{C+1}^\top A_i=\mathbf 1_{h_i}^\top,
\end{equation}
so each native output coordinate maps to exactly one aligned coordinate, while multiple native coordinates may map to the same target class.
The coordinator then computes the aligned probability vector
\begin{equation}
q_i(x)=A_i p_i(x)\in\Delta^{C},
\label{eq:aligned_output}
\end{equation}
where $\Delta^C$ is the probability simplex with $C+1$ coordinates.
The first $C$ coordinates correspond to the predefined target classes.
We reserve the final coordinate, denoted by $\bot$, for probability mass that cannot be mapped to any target class.
By keeping this unmapped mass explicitly, later evaluation penalizes it instead of silently dropping it.
We refer to $q_i(x)$ as candidate $i$'s aligned score vector for record $x$.
For a record set $X=\{x_t\}_{t=1}^{|X|}$, we stack these vectors as
\begin{equation}
Q_i(X)=\big[q_i(x_t)^\top\big]_{t=1}^{|X|}
\in\mathbb R^{|X|\times(C+1)},
\label{eq:aligned_score_block}
\end{equation}
which we call the aligned score block of candidate $i$ on $X$.

After making the detector outputs comparable, we use held-out deployment records to determine whether each pretrained detector remains useful under the deployment distribution.
Before selection, the coordinator therefore holds a labeled calibration set $\mathcal D^{\rm cal}$ from the deployment domain.
These records are used only to evaluate the pretrained candidate pool and are disjoint from the records later used to fit the shared stacker and from the sealed test set.
Every detector, semantic map, and source class-count vector is fixed before calibration begins.
The test labels remain hidden until both detector selection and stacker fitting are complete, so they cannot influence detector selection or stacker fitting.

We next model the selection cost of choosing a detector.
A selected detector must be delivered for use, and its aligned score block must later be uploaded for shared stacker fitting.
We combine these two communication terms into a single selection cost measured in logical bytes.
Here, logical bytes count application-level payload under the modeled workflow and exclude transport headers and implementation-specific protocol overhead.
Let $B_t$ denote the total number of stacker-fitting records across all contributors, and let $d$ denote the logical byte width assigned to one uploaded scalar, including either a probability value or a public label.
For a selected set $S$, we define
\begin{equation}
C_{\mathrm{sel}}(S)=\sum_{i\in S}b_i,
\qquad
b_i=J K_i+dB_t(C+1).
\label{eq:candidate_cost}
\end{equation}
The first term in $b_i$ accounts for delivering candidate $i$ to the $J$ contributors, while the second accounts for uploading the aligned score block over all stacker-fitting records.

Given a budget fraction $\beta\in(0,1]$, we set the hard selection-cost budget to
\begin{equation}
B=\left\lfloor\beta\sum_{i=1}^{M}b_i\right\rfloor.
\label{eq:logical_budget}
\end{equation}
The reference cost includes the entire candidate pool, including candidates that may later fail calibration.
We additionally count the initial candidate upload and the stacker-label upload:
\begin{equation}
C_{\mathrm{total}}(S)=\sum_{i=1}^{M}K_i+C_{\mathrm{sel}}(S)+dB_t.
\label{eq:total_logical_communication}
\end{equation}
We use $C_{\mathrm{sel}}$ to enforce the selection-cost budget and $C_{\mathrm{total}}$ to compare the overall logical communication of \Fname with that of the full detector pool.
This accounting covers integration and stacker fitting; distribution of the fitted stacker and subsequent inference traffic are outside the modeled communication cost.

\subsection{Threat Model}

We consider the privacy risk introduced when contributors upload detector scores for shared stacker fitting.
Although raw historical source records are unavailable and never transferred
during integration, the prediction scores computed from stacker-fitting
records may still reveal information about those records.
We therefore consider an honest-but-curious coordinator that follows the prescribed integration
protocol but may attempt to infer record-level information from the uploaded
scores.

We assume that each contributed detector, its semantic map, and its coarse
source class counts are fixed and honestly reported before selection.
Record labels used for stacker fitting are treated as public.
When score protection is enabled, each contributor randomizes its selected score blocks locally before transmission, so the privacy guarantee does not rely on the coordinator performing the randomization.
Our guarantee protects the feature-dependent information contained in these
randomized score uploads; it does not protect the public labels, contributed
detector parameters, or source class-count metadata.
Malicious contributors that poison detector parameters, forge semantic maps or source class counts, or deviate from the prescribed randomization mechanism are outside our scope, as we focus on record-level privacy of score uploads.

\subsection{Detector Selection Objective}

The aligned outputs let us compare detectors in a common label space, but the budget may still prevent us from deploying all of them.
We therefore need to decide which subset to keep.
Two considerations matter: we want the selected set to preserve source classes represented across the source domains, and we also want its detectors to remain useful in the deployment domain.
These goals can favor different candidates.
For example, one detector may be the only affordable model that covers a rare source class but have only moderate deployment-domain performance, while another may exhibit strong deployment-domain performance but add no new source-class coverage.

We handle this conflict with a strict priority: weighted source class coverage is maximized first under the selection-cost budget, and deployment-domain predictive behavior is compared only after that maximum is fixed.
This is a lexicographic objective: the secondary objective can improve the choice among equally well-covered sets, but it can never compensate for losing primary coverage.
We express this priority as
\begin{equation}
\operatorname{lexmax}_{\varnothing\ne S\subseteq\widehat{\mathcal R}}
\left(C_{\mathrm{src}}(S),\Phi(S)\right)
\quad\text{s.t.}\quad
\sum_{i\in S}b_i\le B.
\label{eq:lexicographic_target}
\end{equation}
Here, $\widehat{\mathcal R}$ is the pool that passes calibration, $C_{\mathrm{src}}$ is the weighted source class coverage defined later, and $\Phi$ summarizes deployment-domain predictive behavior.
We solve the maximum source-coverage problem with a bitmask dynamic program and then apply coverage-preserving local refinement to improve deployment-domain predictive behavior.
Section~\ref{sec:method_analysis} characterizes this refinement and explains why a single fixed weighted sum cannot enforce the same priority across arbitrary candidate pools.


\section{Design of \Fname}
\label{sec:approach}


\subsection{Overview of \Fname}

We now describe how \Fname realizes the preceding problem formulation.
Figure~\ref{fig:workflow} summarizes the workflow in four steps.
In Step~1, we use the semantic map $A_i$ defined in the system model to transform each detector's native probabilities $p_i(x)$ into the aligned representation $q_i(x)$, so that heterogeneous detector outputs can be compared in a common label space.
We also retain the source class-count metadata and selection cost introduced earlier for subsequent selection.
These source counts are later converted into the source-support information used by the primary objective.

With the detector outputs made comparable, Step~2 uses held-out calibration records to evaluate how the pretrained detectors perform in the deployment domain.
We use an upper confidence bound on Brier risk to determine whether each detector enters the calibration-eligible pool, while retaining additional classwise utility and prediction-pattern statistics for later refinement.
Based on this eligible pool, Step~3 first uses the source-support information and detector selection costs to find the maximum weighted source class coverage allowed by the selection-cost budget.
We then use the retained calibration statistics to improve deployment-domain predictive behavior without reducing the achieved coverage.
Finally, once the selected detector set is fixed, Step~4 uses only its aligned score blocks to fit one shared stacker and keeps the same selected layout for subsequent sealed-test inference.
The following subsections define the operations in these four steps in detail.

\subsection{Selection and Stacking Workflow}

Algorithm~\ref{alg:silostitch} summarizes the four steps above.
Let $j$ index contributors and $i$ index candidate detectors, and write $\mathcal D_j^{\rm stk}=(X_j^{\rm stk},Y_j^{\rm stk})$ for contributor $j$'s stacker-fitting records and labels.
The calibration partition is first used to evaluate the pretrained candidates, form the eligible pool, and compute the deployment-domain statistics used for refinement.
The primary solver then selects from this pool using the fixed source metadata and selection costs.
We then refine one primary-optimal solution using the retained calibration statistics.
Only after the selected set $\widehat S$ is fixed do we use the independent stacker-fitting records to train the shared stacker.
This separation prevents the records used to fit the stacker from also influencing which detector outputs become its input features.

The contributors then evaluate only the selected detectors on their stacker-fitting records.
If score protection is enabled, they randomize the selected aligned score blocks locally before upload; otherwise the same workflow is used without added noise.

\subsection{Brier-Based Evaluation and Screening}

After aligning every candidate to the same target label space through $q_i(x)$, we evaluate the aligned outputs on held-out deployment records to obtain comparable statistics for screening and refinement.
For this assessment, we use a full-vector Brier score because it is bounded, evaluates the complete aligned probability vector, and naturally penalizes probability mass assigned to the unmapped $\bot$ coordinate.

For calibration record $(x_t,y_t)$, let $e_{y_t}\in\mathbb R^{C+1}$ be the one-hot label vector with a zero $\bot$ coordinate.
For candidate $i$, we define
\begin{equation}
\ell_{i,t}=\frac12\left\|q_i(x_t)-e_{y_t}\right\|_2^2,
\qquad
g_{i,t}=1-\ell_{i,t}\in[0,1].
\label{eq:brier_utility}
\end{equation}
Thus, $g_{i,t}$ measures the quality of the whole aligned prediction rather than one coordinate at a time.
If a detector places probability on an unmapped output, that probability remains in the vector and lowers the utility.

\bheading{Eligibility screening}
We first determine whether each candidate detector has sufficiently low deployment-domain risk to remain eligible.
Using only its empirical mean can be misleading when the calibration set is finite, so we screen with an upper confidence bound on deployment-domain Brier risk.
With $N$ calibration records and failure probability $\delta\in(0,1)$, we compute
\begin{equation}
\widehat\mu_i=\frac1N\sum_{t=1}^{N}g_{i,t},
\qquad
r=\sqrt{\frac{\log(4M/\delta)}{2N}},
\label{eq:overall_calibration}
\end{equation}
and define
\begin{equation}
R_i^+=1-\widehat\mu_i+r.
\label{eq:risk_gate}
\end{equation}
We keep candidate $i$ only if $R_i^+\le\tau_L$, giving the calibration-eligible pool
\begin{equation}
\widehat{\mathcal R}=\{i:R_i^+\le\tau_L\}.
\label{eq:eligible_pool}
\end{equation}
In other words, eligibility depends on both the observed deployment-domain performance and the uncertainty caused by finite calibration data.

\bheading{Classwise scores}
An acceptable average risk does not guarantee that a detector remains useful for every target class.
We therefore keep a conservative classwise utility estimate for each detector--class pair and use these scores later when we refine sets that already achieve maximum source class coverage.
For class $c$, let
\begin{equation}
N_c=\sum_t\mathbf 1\{y_t=c\},
\end{equation}
and define
\begin{equation}
\widehat\mu_{i,c}=\frac1{N_c}\sum_{t:y_t=c}g_{i,t}.
\end{equation}
We require every calibration class to appear at least once, so $N_c>0$.
We then compute
\begin{equation}
r_c=\sqrt{\frac{\log(4MC/\delta)}{2N_c}},
\qquad
a_{i,c}=\left[\widehat\mu_{i,c}-r_c\right]_+,
\label{eq:class_lower_utility}
\end{equation}
where $[z]_+=\max\{0,z\}$.
The value $a_{i,c}$ is a lower confidence bound on the deployment-domain utility of candidate $i$ on class $c$.

\bheading{Prediction similarity}
Two detectors can have similar average utility and still make nearly identical predictions.
Selecting both may then consume budget without adding much new predictive information.
To capture this redundancy, we compare the aligned outputs of the candidates on a fixed class-stratified set of calibration indices $\mathcal A\subseteq\{1,\ldots,N\}$.
We form
\begin{equation}
z_i=\operatorname{vec}\left([q_i(x_t)]_{t\in\mathcal A}\right),
\qquad
\kappa_{i,k}=\left[
\frac{\langle z_i,z_k\rangle}{\|z_i\|_2\|z_k\|_2}
\right]_{0}^{1}.
\label{eq:behavior_similarity}
\end{equation}
Here, $[z]_0^1=\min\{1,\max\{0,z\}\}$ and the clipping only removes numerical roundoff outside $[0,1]$.
Later, we use these similarities in a representativeness term: rather than rewarding pairwise difference for its own sake, we ask whether the selected set contains detectors whose prediction patterns represent the wider eligible pool.

\subsection{Coverage-Prioritized Detector Selection}

The calibration statistics tell us how the pretrained detectors perform in the deployment domain, but they do not tell us which historical source classes would disappear if particular detectors were removed.
That information comes from the coarse source class counts retained with the candidate models.
We therefore use source class coverage as the primary objective and use the calibration statistics only after the best achievable coverage has been fixed.

\bheading{Weighted source class coverage}
Recall that $H_{i,c}$ denotes candidate $i$'s mapped source count for target class $c$.
A very small count may provide weak evidence that a class was meaningfully represented during training, so we first convert the count into a support indicator.
For a minimum support threshold $n_{\min}>0$, define
\begin{equation}
\Gamma_{i,c}=\mathbf 1\{H_{i,c}\ge n_{\min}\}.
\label{eq:semantic_presence}
\end{equation}
Thus, $\Gamma_{i,c}=1$ indicates that candidate $i$ has sufficient source support for class $c$ to count toward the primary objective. Let $\boldsymbol\Gamma_i=(\Gamma_{i,1},\ldots,\Gamma_{i,C})$ denote candidate $i$'s source-support vector.

We also give more weight to source classes that are scarce across the whole candidate pool.
Let $H_c=\sum_iH_{i,c}$ denote the aggregate source count of class $c$, and let $\mathcal C_+=\{c:H_c>0\}$ denote the source-supported classes.
We assume $\mathcal C_+\ne\varnothing$ and set
\begin{equation}
w_c=
\begin{cases}
\displaystyle
\frac{H_c^{-1/2}}{\sum_{c'\in\mathcal C_+}H_{c'}^{-1/2}},
& c\in\mathcal C_+,\\[2mm]
0, & c\notin\mathcal C_+,
\end{cases}
\qquad
\sum_{c=1}^{C}w_c=1.
\label{eq:semantic_weights}
\end{equation}
The inverse-square-root rule gives greater weight to less represented source-supported classes while moderating frequency differences relative to inverse-frequency weighting.

For selected set $S$, we define weighted source class coverage as
\begin{equation}
C_{\mathrm{src}}(S)=
\sum_{c=1}^{C}w_c
\mathbf 1\{\exists i\in S:\Gamma_{i,c}=1\}.
\label{eq:source_coverage}
\end{equation}
A class contributes once as soon as at least one selected detector provides sufficient source support for it; selecting more detectors for the same class does not increase the primary value.
Therefore, $C_{\mathrm{src}}$ measures how much historical source class coverage is retained by the selected set, not how accurately that set predicts deployment records.

\bheading{Secondary predictive objective}
We consider the feasible case in which at least one calibration-eligible detector fits the selection-cost budget. 
After the primary coverage value is fixed, several feasible detector sets may still attain exactly the same coverage.
We compare such sets using three secondary objective components that capture different aspects of deployment-domain prediction quality: record-level utility, classwise utility, and prediction-pattern representativeness.
With $h_\tau(z)=1-e^{-z/\tau}$, define
\begin{align}
U(S)
&=\frac1N\sum_{t=1}^{N}
 h_{\tau_U}\!\left(\sum_{i\in S}g_{i,t}\right),
\nonumber\\
C_{\mathrm{pred}}(S)
&=\frac1C\sum_{c=1}^{C}
 h_{\tau_C}\!\left(\sum_{i\in S}a_{i,c}\right),
\nonumber\\
D_{\mathrm{rep}}(S)
&=\frac1{|\widehat{\mathcal R}|}
\sum_{k\in\widehat{\mathcal R}}
\max_{i\in S}\kappa_{i,k}.
\label{eq:secondary_components}
\end{align}
The term $U(S)$ rewards sets whose members perform well across the calibration records.
The term $C_{\mathrm{pred}}(S)$ keeps attention on classwise utility by using the conservative scores $a_{i,c}$.
The term $D_{\mathrm{rep}}(S)$ rewards sets whose prediction patterns represent those observed across the eligible pool.

The saturating functions in $U$ and $C_{\mathrm{pred}}$ impose diminishing returns.
Once several selected detectors already perform well on the same record or class, another similar detector contributes less.
This makes it harder for redundant prediction patterns to dominate the secondary comparison.
We define $\max_{i\in\varnothing}\kappa_{i,k}=0$ and combine the three terms as
\begin{equation}
\Phi(S)=\lambda_U U(S)+\lambda_C C_{\mathrm{pred}}(S)
+\lambda_D D_{\mathrm{rep}}(S),
\label{eq:secondary_objective}
\end{equation}
where $\tau_U,\tau_C>0$, $\lambda_U,\lambda_C,\lambda_D\ge0$, and $\lambda_U+\lambda_C+\lambda_D=1$.
We use $\Phi$ only after the primary coverage value has been fixed, so no gain in deployment-domain predictive behavior can compensate for a loss in source class coverage.

\bheading{Budgeted selection procedure}
We now solve the primary problem: among the calibration-eligible candidates, what is the largest weighted source class coverage that fits within the selection-cost budget?
Define
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal F_B&=\left\{
\varnothing\ne S\subseteq\widehat{\mathcal R}:
\sum_{i\in S}b_i\le B
\right\},\\
W^\star&=\max_{S\in\mathcal F_B}C_{\mathrm{src}}(S).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:primary_value}
\end{equation}
Because $C_{\mathrm{src}}$ depends only on which classes are covered, we encode every source-support vector $\boldsymbol\Gamma_i$ as a $C$-bit mask and run a sparse dynamic program over reachable masks.
For each mask, we retain only the least-cost detector set that produces it.
Any more expensive set with the same mask is dominated because adding any future detector produces the same updated union mask from either set.

The dynamic program returns both the exact optimum $W^\star$ and a least-cost set $S_0$ that attains it.
Starting from this least-cost exact solution leaves as much unused budget as possible for improving deployment-domain predictive behavior.
With a tolerance $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{loc}}\geq 0$, we first consider additional candidates according to their secondary gain per byte and accept an addition only when its gain exceeds $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{loc}}$.
We then examine one-for-one replacements and accept a swap only when it remains budget-feasible, keeps coverage equal to $W^\star$, and improves $\Phi$ by more than the same tolerance.
Every accepted refinement therefore preserves $C_{\mathrm{src}}=W^\star$ while improving $\Phi$.

\subsection{Shared Stacker Learning}

Once selection fixes $\widehat S$, only the aligned outputs of the selected detectors enter the shared stacker.
We use the same fixed detector order for stacker fitting and inference.
For input $x$, we define the selected representation
\begin{equation}
\phi_{\widehat S}(x)=
\operatorname{concat}_{i\in\widehat S}q_i(x)
\in\mathbb R^{|\widehat S|(C+1)}.
\label{eq:selected_representation}
\end{equation}
The selected detector set determines which score blocks are used, while this fixed order determines their feature-column positions.

\bheading{Local score perturbation}
Although raw historical source records are unavailable, the scores computed from stacker-fitting records may still reveal information about those records.
When score protection is enabled, each contributor therefore randomizes every coordinate of each selected detector score vector before upload:
\begin{equation}
\widetilde q_i(x_t)=q_i(x_t)+\eta_{i,t},
\qquad
\eta_{i,t,k}\overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim}
\operatorname{Lap}\!\left(0,\frac{2}{\epsilon_b}\right).
\label{eq:local_laplace_release}
\end{equation}
The resulting stacker input is
\begin{equation}
\widetilde\phi_{\widehat S}(x)=
\operatorname{concat}_{i\in\widehat S}\widetilde q_i(x).
\label{eq:private_selected_representation}
\end{equation}
Section~\ref{sec:method_analysis} later proves the corresponding label-conditional local privacy guarantee and its composition across selected detector score vectors.

\bheading{Shared stacker fitting}
Let $\mathcal D^{\mathrm{stk}}=\biguplus_{j=1}^{J}\mathcal D_j^{\mathrm{stk}}$ denote the collection of contributor-held stacker-fitting records.
After selection, contributors compute the selected score features locally, and the coordinator uses the uploaded features and labels to fit one shared classifier:
\begin{equation}
\widehat f_{\widehat S}=\mathsf{Fit}_{\mathrm{global}}
\left(
\{(\widetilde\phi_{\widehat S}(x_t),y_t):
(x_t,y_t)\in\mathcal D^{\mathrm{stk}}\}
\right).
\label{eq:shared_stacker}
\end{equation}
For the nonprivate configuration, we simply set $\widetilde\phi_{\widehat S}=\phi_{\widehat S}$.
Because both configurations use only the columns indexed by $\widehat S$, unselected detectors cannot influence the learned stacker.

In a distributed realization of the stacker-fitting step, contributors generate the Laplace noise locally before transmission.
Our centralized privacy experiment samples the same distribution only to isolate the utility effect of score randomization.
For sealed-test inference, we keep the selected detector set and aligned feature layout fixed and reuse the fitted shared stacker without retraining any contributed detector.

\section{Theoretical Analysis}
\label{sec:method_analysis}

We state the guarantees that directly support the workflow: label-conditional privacy of score uploads, post-selection validity of the calibration bounds, exactness of the primary solver, and coverage preservation of local refinement. Proofs and the additional mixture-shift analysis appear in Appendix~\ref{app:theory}.

\subsection{Local Privacy of Score Uploads}

Recall that the shared stacker is trained from selected detector scores rather than from the historical source records.
Because these scores are still record dependent, we ask what information the contributor-side Laplace mechanism protects.
We use the label-conditional adjacency relation defined earlier: the record label is public, and two neighboring records $(x,y)$ and $(x',y)$ differ only in their feature vectors.

Before any stacker-fitting record is processed, we fix the selected detector set and all semantic maps.
For a batched upload, neighboring batches have the same size, the same row alignment, and the same sequence of public labels, but they differ in one feature vector.
Each contributor samples fresh noise independently across records, selected detector score vectors, and output coordinates.
Under this execution model, we obtain the following guarantee.

\begin{theorem}[Label-conditional local score privacy]
\label{thm:local_score_privacy}
Suppose that the selected detectors and their semantic maps are fixed and that $0<\epsilon_b<\infty$, and suppose that each contributor uses fresh private randomness in Eq.~\ref{eq:local_laplace_release}.
Then one released detector score vector satisfies $(\epsilon_b,0)$-local differential privacy under the preceding label-conditional adjacency relation.
If $s=|\widehat S|$ detector score vectors are released for the same record, their joint release satisfies $(s\epsilon_b,0)$-local differential privacy.
Under one-record replacement, releasing the entire contributor batch has the same record-level guarantee.
More generally, assigning parameter $\epsilon_i$ to detector score vector $i$ gives $(\sum_{i\in\widehat S}\epsilon_i,0)$-local differential privacy.
\end{theorem}

% The proof is given in Appendix~\ref{app:proof_privacy}. % repeated so delete

The theorem also clarifies how the privacy budget interacts with detector selection.
If we want one total per-record score budget $\epsilon$, we can assign $\epsilon_b=\epsilon/s$ to each of the $s$ selected detector score vectors.
The corresponding noise scale becomes $2s/\epsilon$.
Thus, under a fixed total score-privacy budget, selecting fewer detector score vectors also reduces the noise required for each released vector.
The guarantee applies only to the randomized score upload, and privacy loss composes again if the same record is released in repeated rounds.

\subsection{Calibration under Heterogeneous Contributors}

The privacy result concerns the stacker-fitting partition.
Detector selection, in contrast, depends on the separate calibration partition introduced in the system model.
We therefore ask a different question here: after we use the calibration statistics to choose a detector subset, can we still trust the confidence bounds computed before selection?

The calibration records may come from different contributors and classes, so we do not assume that every record follows one identical distribution.
Instead, let $j_t$ identify the contributor associated with calibration record $t$, and condition on the observed contributor--label design
\begin{equation}
\mathcal I=\{(j_t,y_t)\}_{t=1}^{N}
\end{equation}
and on the pretrained candidate pool $\mathcal Q$.
Let $X_t$ denote the random feature vector for calibration record $t$, and define
\begin{equation}
G_{i,t}=1-\frac12\left\|q_i(X_t)-e_{y_t}\right\|_2^2\in[0,1],
\end{equation}
whose observed realization is $g_{i,t}$ in Eq.~\ref{eq:brier_utility}.
Conditioned on $(\mathcal Q,\mathcal I)$, we assume the records are independent, while allowing their conditional distributions to differ across records.
We define
\begin{equation}
\mu_i=\frac1N\sum_{t=1}^{N}\mathbb E[G_{i,t}\mid\mathcal Q,\mathcal I],
\qquad
R_i=1-\mu_i,
\label{eq:heterogeneous_overall_mean}
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
\mu_{i,c}=\frac1{N_c}\sum_{t:y_t=c}
\mathbb E[G_{i,t}\mid\mathcal Q,\mathcal I].
\label{eq:heterogeneous_class_mean}
\end{equation}
These quantities average performance over the actual mix of calibration records observed by the coordinator; they are not equal-contributor averages or worst-contributor guarantees.

\begin{theorem}[Simultaneous calibration]
\label{thm:simultaneous_calibration}
Under the preceding assumptions, with probability at least $1-\delta$, the following inequalities hold simultaneously for every candidate $i$ and class $c$:
\begin{align}
R_i&\le R_i^+\le R_i+2r,
\label{eq:simultaneous_risk}\\
\max\{0,\mu_{i,c}-2r_c\}
&\le a_{i,c}\le\mu_{i,c}.
\label{eq:simultaneous_class}
\end{align}
Consequently, these inequalities remain valid for every candidate in any set selected adaptively from the calibration statistics.
\end{theorem}

The same event also bounds the effect of calibration screening on feasible source coverage.
Appendix~\ref{app:screening_coverage} gives the corresponding coverage sandwich.

\subsection{Exact Primary Coverage under a Hard Budget}

Once calibration fixes the eligible pool, the primary selection problem has a simple interpretation.
Each detector has a selection cost and covers a subset of source classes, while the hard budget may prevent us from selecting all detectors at once.
We first characterize the difficulty of this problem and then show that the bitmask dynamic program used by \Fname returns the exact primary optimum.

\begin{theorem}[Primary hardness and exact parameterized solver]
\label{thm:primary_solver}
Computing $W^\star$ is NP-hard, even when every candidate is eligible, every cost is one, and all class weights are uniform.
Nevertheless, the bitmask dynamic program returns a nonempty feasible set $S_0$ such that
\begin{equation}
C_{\mathrm{src}}(S_0)=W^\star,
\end{equation}
with minimum total selection cost among the sets attaining $W^\star$.
It stores at most $2^C$ masks and examines at most $|\widehat{\mathcal R}|2^C$ state transitions.
Thus, the primary stage is fixed-parameter tractable in the number of target classes $C$.
In other words, the transition bound is linear in the number of eligible detectors but exponential in the number of target classes.
\end{theorem}

\subsection{Secondary Objective and Local Refinement}

The exact primary solver fixes the best source coverage that the budget can support, but many detector sets may still attain that same value.
We use the secondary objective $\Phi$ to choose among them.
Before stating the local-refinement guarantee, we first explain why we do not replace the ordered objective with one weighted sum.

A fixed weighted sum cannot enforce the same priority uniformly across deployments, while the secondary objective is normalized, monotone, and submodular for fixed calibration statistics.
Appendix~\ref{app:secondary_structure} provides formal statements and proofs.

This submodularity does not by itself give a global approximation guarantee for the deployed secondary refinement.
The family of sets that preserve exact primary coverage,
\begin{equation}
\mathcal F^\star=\{S\in\mathcal F_B:C_{\mathrm{src}}(S)=W^\star\},
\end{equation}
does not have the simple structure required by standard submodular-knapsack results: removing a detector can destroy the required coverage, and an arbitrary exchange need not restore it.
Therefore, familiar global guarantees such as $1-1/e$ do not directly apply after we require exact primary coverage.

For $S\in\mathcal F^\star$, let $\mathcal N_1(S)$ contain every set obtained by removing one selected candidate and adding one unselected candidate while preserving budget feasibility and coverage $W^\star$.
We can then state a local certificate for a refinement that reaches a one-swap fixed point.

\begin{theorem}[Coverage preservation and one-swap certificate]
\label{thm:local_refinement}
Let the refinement start from a primary-optimal set $S_0\in\mathcal F^\star$ and accept only the budget-feasible additions and coverage-preserving replacements described above. Every set produced by the refinement remains in $\mathcal F^\star$. Moreover, if the replacement phase ends with a complete pass over all budget-feasible, coverage-preserving one-for-one replacements during which no swap improves $\Phi$ by more than $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{loc}}$, then its output $\widehat S$ satisfies
\begin{equation}
\Phi(S')\le\Phi(\widehat S)+\varepsilon_{\mathrm{loc}},
\qquad
\forall S'\in\mathcal N_1(\widehat S).
\label{eq:one_swap_certificate}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

The proof is given in Appendix~\ref{app:proof_refinement}.

The refinement examines additions and one-for-one replacements rather than all possible multi-detector exchanges. It preserves the primary optimum throughout. If the replacement phase reaches the termination condition in Theorem~\ref{thm:local_refinement}, the returned set additionally satisfies the stated one-swap certificate.

Historical source coverage does not by itself imply deployment-domain classwise utility.
Appendix~\ref{app:mixture_shift} gives a post-selection analysis under stable contributor--class mean Brier risk and bounded contributor-mixture shift.

\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:experiments}

We evaluate whether \Fname preserves source class coverage and deployment-domain detection quality while reducing total logical communication, and examine its robustness.
We organize the evaluation around five questions.
\begin{packeditemize}
    \item \textbf{RQ1: Detection quality under a budget.}
    How much source coverage and deployment-domain detection quality are retained under a limited selection-cost budget?

    \item \textbf{RQ2: Contributor label heterogeneity.}
    How does contributor label heterogeneity affect candidate construction, source coverage, and detection quality?

    \item \textbf{RQ3: Selection components and learners.}
    How sensitive is selection to the secondary objective, source-coverage settings, and learner backends?

    \item \textbf{RQ4: Score randomization.}
    What utility and security costs arise from stronger Laplace score randomization?

    \item \textbf{RQ5: Scalability and runtime.}
    Does the primary solver match exhaustive search where feasible, remain practical as candidate and class dimensions grow, and reduce communication at the evaluated scale?
\end{packeditemize}

\bheading{Detection quality and source coverage}
At the 50\% operating point, \Fname consumes 49.5\% to 50.0\% of the full-pool selection cost.
Across all four tasks, its absolute mean macro-F1 gap from the full detector pool remains below 0.028.
The gaps are at most 0.0052 on MalDroid, Darknet, and DoH, while DDoS has the largest gap at about 0.026.
Thus, even the largest observed loss remains small relative to the roughly one-half reduction in selection cost.

The budget sweep in Fig.~\ref{fig:eval-budget-utility} shows that a larger selection-cost budget does not guarantee higher sealed-test macro F1 on every dataset.
MalDroid improves most clearly as the budget increases, DoH changes only slightly, and DDoS and Darknet are non-monotonic.

We next examine whether the communication savings degrade performance on the weakest class.
Relative to the full pool, the 50\% selected set changes accuracy by at most 0.0046 and minimum evaluated class recall by at most 0.0061 on MalDroid, Darknet, and DoH.
On DDoS, the accuracy gap is 0.0296 but the minimum evaluated class-recall gap is only 0.0094.
Darknet remains weak even under the full-pool reference, which reaches 0.0077 minimum evaluated class recall compared with 0.0058 after selection.
Most importantly for our primary objective, \Fname attains the primary optimum $W^\star$ under the 50\% budget on all four datasets.

\bheading{Why coverage priority matters}
The comparison with individual-utility ranking illustrates why deployment-domain average performance should not be optimized alone.
On MalDroid and Darknet, \Fname has the higher mean macro F1, while the two methods are nearly identical on DoH.
DDoS exposes the intended conflict most clearly: the utility-only baseline gains 0.0150 macro F1 over \Fname, but its weighted source class coverage falls to 0.910, whereas \Fname retains the maximum value of 1.0.
In other words, the utility-only selector achieves a small deployment-domain predictive gain at the cost of reduced historical source class coverage.
Our ordered objective explicitly rules out that exchange.

Overall, RQ1 shows the deployment advantage targeted by the paper: at roughly half the selection cost, we attain the primary source-coverage optimum and keep macro-F1 loss below 0.028 across all four tasks.
The DDoS comparison also provides a concrete case in which the source-coverage priority is active rather than merely formal.

\subsection{RQ2: Contributor Label Heterogeneity}

The previous experiment starts from a fixed source candidate pool.
We now study how the heterogeneity that creates such a pool can affect the integration problem before and after selection.
We separate two effects: whether we can construct valid multiclass source detectors at all, and, when we can, whether stronger source-label imbalance consistently reduces the final macro F1.

We retain the original DDoS and Darknet contributor assignments for the original-layout analysis. For the controlled heterogeneity study, we generate Darknet and MalDroid partitions from Dirichlet distributions with $\alpha\in\{10,1,0.5,0.1\}$, together with an IID allocation.
Smaller $\alpha$ creates stronger label imbalance.
For the controlled partitions, we require every accepted source contributor to contain at least two training classes so that its pretrained candidate detector remains multiclass.
Figure~\ref{fig:eval-heterogeneity} reports both partition feasibility and the resulting detection quality.

Darknet remains feasible for all ten seeds at every evaluated heterogeneity level, even as the measured contributor-label divergence rises from 0.0008 under IID allocation to 0.3516 at $\alpha=0.1$.
MalDroid behaves differently because it has only five source classes spread across the same 66 contributors.

For these extreme settings, the difficulty arises before selective integration: under the evaluated generation procedure, no valid multiclass candidate pool was obtained.

\bheading{Quality after a valid pool exists}
Conditional on a usable candidate pool, stronger heterogeneity does not produce a monotonic decline in macro F1.
Darknet stays between 0.5895 and 0.5976 at the 50\% budget, while MalDroid stays between 0.8328 and 0.8375 over the feasible range.
These narrow descriptive ranges suggest that, in this controlled experiment, increasing source-label imbalance affects candidate-pool feasibility more clearly than it affects aggregate detection quality once a valid pool has already been constructed.

The original contributor layouts show a related effect at the class level.
As the budget increases from 25\% to 75\%, Darknet macro F1 rises from 0.5830 to 0.5903.
For DDoS, macro F1 changes from 0.6039 to 0.6104 over the same budget range, while minimum class recall increases from 0.0568 to 0.0773.
Thus, additional selection-cost budget can be more visible in the weakest observed class than in an aggregate score.

\bheading{When the coverage priority is non-binding}
We also remove only the explicit source-coverage priority while keeping the same secondary refinement.
Across 30 comparisons on the original assignments and 225 comparisons on the controlled assignments, the two variants choose the same set and therefore obtain the same macro F1, minimum class recall, communication, and source coverage.
This is the complementary case to DDoS in RQ1: when the secondary search already reaches the maximum available source coverage, the hard priority is non-binding; when a utility-oriented selector would trade coverage away, the priority changes the result and prevents that loss.

\subsection{RQ3: Selection Components and Learners}

After the primary coverage value has been fixed, \Fname still needs to choose among coverage-equivalent detector sets and then fit the shared stacker.
RQ3 therefore separates design choices at these two levels.
We first remove the three secondary objective components one at a time, then vary the source-coverage settings, and finally compare the source detector and stacker backends.

\bheading{Secondary objective components}
Recall that $U$ measures record-level utility, $C_{\mathrm{pred}}$ measures conservative classwise utility, and $D_{\mathrm{rep}}$ measures how well the selected set represents the prediction patterns of the eligible pool.
In every ablation, we keep the primary source class coverage fixed at its optimum and remove only one of these secondary terms.
At the 50\% budget, removing $U$ lowers DDoS macro F1 by 0.0190, while removing $D_{\mathrm{rep}}$ raises it by 0.0181.
Across datasets and budgets, however, the direction of these changes is not consistent, and no single removal remains statistically significant after Holm correction.
We therefore interpret these terms as tie-breakers among coverage-equivalent sets rather than as individually universal sources of accuracy gain.

\bheading{Source-coverage settings}
We next vary the minimum source count $n_{\min}\in\{1,2,5,10\}$ and replace the inverse-square-root class weights with uniform weights.
Across two fixed Darknet layouts and two learner combinations, these changes leave the selected set, attained source coverage, macro F1, and minimum evaluated class recall unchanged.
The tested Darknet result is therefore insensitive to these values of $n_{\min}$ and to the two evaluated class-weighting rules.

\subsection{RQ5: Scalability and Runtime}


Theorem~\ref{thm:primary_solver} shows that the state space of the primary dynamic program grows exponentially with the number of target classes $C$.
RQ5 therefore checks three practical questions: whether the implementation matches exhaustive search when enumeration is possible, how runtime changes as the candidate and class dimensions grow, and what reduction in total logical communication we obtain at the evaluated scale.

\bheading{Exactness audit}
We first compare the dynamic program with exhaustive search at $C=8$ and $M\in\{8,10,12,16\}$.
Across ten repetitions at each candidate-pool size, the solver attains the same primary coverage as exhaustive search in all 40 comparisons.
Its median runtime ranges from 37.70 to 118.72~ms, and every 95th percentile remains below 125~ms.
This audit checks the implementation against an enumerable ground truth; the larger sweeps below characterize runtime at greater scale.



\bheading{Scaling behavior}
We next vary the two problem dimensions separately in Fig.~\ref{fig:eval-solver-scaling}.
With the class count fixed at 12, increasing the candidate pool from 66 to 256 raises the median selection time from 1.99 to 33.73~s.
With 66 candidates fixed, increasing the class count from 12 to 20 raises the median from 2.95 to 100.10~s.
The second increase is much sharper, which matches the structure of the bitmask solver: the candidate count enters linearly in the transition bound, while the class count controls the $2^C$ mask space.
Thus, within the evaluated range, runtime is more sensitive to the class count than to the candidate-pool sizes considered here.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:discussion_conclusion}

We study selective integration of pretrained intrusion detectors after raw historical source records become unavailable.
\Fname enables budget-aware reuse by aligning heterogeneous outputs, preserving source class coverage, and integrating selected detectors with a shared stacker.
Experiments on four datasets show that a 50\% selection-cost budget approximately halves modeled logical communication while keeping detection quality close to the full detector pool.

% The strict priority matters when source coverage and deployment-domain utility conflict: on DDoS, the utility-only baseline gains 0.0150 macro F1 but reduces weighted source class coverage from 1.0 to 0.910, whereas the heterogeneity ablation shows cases where the priority is non-binding because the secondary search already reaches maximum coverage. The primary dynamic program is exact when the full recurrence completes within the implementation cap, local refinement preserves that optimum, and the simultaneous calibration result applies to the observed calibration mixture; the additional mixture-shift analysis assumes stable contributor--class mean Brier risk and bounded mixture change.

\section{Additional Theoretical Analysis}

\subsection{Proof of Local Score Privacy}
\label{app:proof_privacy}
\begin{proof}
Because $q_i(x)$ and $q_i(x')$ are probability vectors, their replacement sensitivity satisfies
\begin{equation}
\|q_i(x)-q_i(x')\|_1\le2.
\end{equation}
Therefore, adding coordinatewise Laplace noise with scale $2/\epsilon_b$ bounds the density ratio of one released detector score vector by $e^{\epsilon_b}$.
Sequential composition over the $s$ selected detector score vectors gives the factor $e^{s\epsilon_b}$.

For a batched upload, neighboring batches differ in only one row.
Since different rows use independent randomization, parallel composition across the unchanged rows does not increase the record-level privacy parameter.
Finally, appending the unchanged public labels does not change the density ratio.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Proof of Simultaneous Calibration}
\label{app:proof_calibration}
\begin{proof}
Conditioned on $(\mathcal Q,\mathcal I)$, Hoeffding's inequality gives
\[
\Pr\{|\widehat\mu_i-\mu_i|>r\mid\mathcal Q,\mathcal I\}
\le2e^{-2Nr^2}=\frac{\delta}{2M}.
\]
A union bound over the $M$ candidates therefore uses at most $\delta/2$ failure probability.
Likewise,
\[
\Pr\{|\widehat\mu_{i,c}-\mu_{i,c}|>r_c\mid\mathcal Q,\mathcal I\}
\le2e^{-2N_cr_c^2}=\frac{\delta}{2MC},
\]
so a second union bound over all $MC$ candidate--class pairs uses the remaining $\delta/2$.
Thus, with probability at least $1-\delta$, all overall and classwise inequalities hold at the same time.

On this event, $1-\widehat\mu_i+r$ lies between $R_i$ and $R_i+2r$, while $\widehat\mu_{i,c}-r_c$ lies between $\mu_{i,c}-2r_c$ and $\mu_{i,c}$.
Applying $[\cdot]_+$ gives the classwise result.
The key point is that this one event covers all $M+MC$ quantities before selection occurs.
We can therefore choose a data-dependent subset without paying another union bound over the $2^M$ possible detector sets.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Effect of Calibration Screening on Source Coverage}
\label{app:screening_coverage}
The same simultaneous event also tells us how the eligibility screen can affect the best source coverage available to the selector.
For any candidate pool $\mathcal E$, let $W(\mathcal E)$ denote the largest budget-feasible weighted source class coverage available from that pool, with value zero if no nonempty set is feasible.
Define
\begin{equation}
\mathcal R^-=\{i:R_i\le\tau_L-2r\},
\qquad
\mathcal R^0=\{i:R_i\le\tau_L\}.
\end{equation}
Then
\begin{equation}
\mathcal R^-\subseteq\widehat{\mathcal R}\subseteq\mathcal R^0,
\qquad
W(\mathcal R^-)\le W(\widehat{\mathcal R})\le W(\mathcal R^0).
\label{eq:coverage_sandwich}
\end{equation}
The left inclusion follows from $R_i^+\le R_i+2r$, while the right inclusion follows from $R_i\le R_i^+$.
Therefore, if one primary-optimal set over $\mathcal R^0$ already lies entirely in $\mathcal R^-$, calibration screening does not reduce the best achievable primary coverage.
This upper-confidence gate therefore provides a finite-sample eligibility screen before budgeted selection.

\subsection{Proof of Primary Hardness and Exactness}
\label{app:proof_primary}
\begin{proof}
For hardness, take a Maximum Coverage instance with universe $\{1,\ldots,C\}$, subsets $\mathcal U_1,\ldots,\mathcal U_M$, and cardinality limit $k$, after removing any element that belongs to no subset. Set $b_i=1$, $B=k$, and $n_{\min}=1$. Let $d_c=|\{i:c\in\mathcal U_i\}|$ and $T=\max_c d_c$. Assign $H_{i,c}=1$ on every incidence and add $T-d_c$ to one incident subset for each class $c$. Then $\Gamma_{i,c}=1$ exactly when $c\in\mathcal U_i$, every aggregate count $H_c$ equals $T$, and hence $w_c=1/C$.
Maximizing $C_{\mathrm{src}}$ then solves that Maximum Coverage instance up to the constant factor $1/C$.

For exactness, process the eligible candidates one at a time.
After the first $t$ candidates, associate every reachable union mask $h$ with the least-cost detector set that produces that mask.
When we process the next candidate, every subset either excludes it and keeps an earlier state, or includes it and extends an earlier mask to $h\vee\boldsymbol\Gamma_{t+1}$.
If two sets produce the same mask, the cheaper one dominates the more expensive one because adding any future candidate produces the same updated union mask from either set.
By induction, the final table therefore contains the least-cost realization of every reachable mask.
Scanning the masks whose costs do not exceed $B$ returns exactly $W^\star$; among masks attaining this value, we choose the least-cost stored realization.

A $C$-bit mask has at most $2^C$ values, and every eligible candidate extends each reachable mask at most once.
If all feasible masks have zero weighted coverage, we return the least-cost feasible singleton so that the downstream stacker still receives a nonempty input without changing the primary value.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Secondary Objective Properties}
\label{app:secondary_structure}
For a fixed problem instance, define the set of achievable primary values as
\begin{equation}
\mathcal V=\{C_{\mathrm{src}}(S):S\in\mathcal F_B\}.
\end{equation}
When $\mathcal V$ contains at least two values, let $\Delta_{\mathrm{inst}}$ be the smallest positive gap between them.
Because $0\le\Phi(S)\le1$, any coefficient $K>1/\Delta_{\mathrm{inst}}$ makes every maximizer of $KC_{\mathrm{src}}+\Phi$ respect the lexicographic order for that one fixed instance.
However, $\Delta_{\mathrm{inst}}$ depends on the class weights and candidate pool and can become arbitrarily small.
Therefore, no universal finite $K$ enforces the required priority across arbitrary deployments.
The ordered formulation in Eq.~\ref{eq:lexicographic_target} states the priority directly and avoids instance-specific scale tuning.

We next ask whether the secondary score has a useful diminishing-returns property: once a selected set already captures a prediction pattern, adding another similar detector should help less.
This standard set-function property is called submodularity.
\begin{proposition}[Secondary structure]
\label{prop:secondary_structure}
For fixed calibration statistics, $U$, $C_{\mathrm{pred}}$, and $D_{\mathrm{rep}}$ are normalized, monotone, submodular set functions.
Consequently, their nonnegative weighted combination $\Phi$ has the same properties.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
For $U$ and $C_{\mathrm{pred}}$, each summand applies the nondecreasing concave function $h_\tau$ to a nonnegative modular sum.
Therefore, the marginal gain from adding the same candidate cannot increase as the selected set grows.
For $D_{\mathrm{rep}}$, eligible detector $k$ contributes $\max_{i\in S}\kappa_{i,k}$, whose marginal gain from adding candidate $a$ is
\[
\max\left\{0,\kappa_{a,k}-\max_{i\in S}\kappa_{i,k}\right\}.
\]
As $S$ grows, the existing maximum can only increase, so this gain can only decrease.
Averaging and taking a nonnegative weighted sum preserve normalization, monotonicity, and submodularity.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Proof of the Local Refinement Guarantee}
\label{app:proof_refinement}
\begin{proof}
The seed $S_0$ belongs to $\mathcal F^\star$. If $S\in\mathcal F^\star$ and $S\cup\{i\}$ is budget feasible, monotonicity gives $C_{\mathrm{src}}(S\cup\{i\})\ge W^\star$, while the definition of $W^\star$ gives the reverse inequality. Hence every accepted addition keeps coverage $W^\star$. Every accepted replacement is explicitly required to be budget feasible and to keep coverage $W^\star$, so all sets produced by the refinement remain in $\mathcal F^\star$.

During the complete final pass, the current set remains $\widehat S$ and every set in $\mathcal N_1(\widehat S)$ is examined. Since no such swap improves $\Phi$ by more than $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{loc}}$, Eq.~\ref{eq:one_swap_certificate} follows.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Coverage Certificates under Contributor Mixture Shift}
\label{app:mixture_shift}

The source-coverage objective records which classes were represented in the historical source domains.
It does not, by itself, guarantee that a detector supporting a class still performs well on that class after deployment.
The held-out calibration records provide calibration evidence, but the contributor mixture observed during deployment may itself differ from the mixture seen during calibration.
We therefore connect historical source support with classwise predictive utility and quantify how a change in contributor mixture can weaken that connection.

This analysis does not change which set \Fname selects.
Instead, after selection, we use the already computed classwise calibration bounds to ask which historically covered classes also have conservative calibration evidence of classwise utility.
For any threshold vector $\boldsymbol u\in\mathbb R^C$, define
\begin{equation}
C_{\mathrm{cert}}(S;\boldsymbol u)=
\sum_{c=1}^{C}w_c
\mathbf 1\{\exists i\in S:
\Gamma_{i,c}=1\ \wedge\ a_{i,c}\ge u_c\}.
\label{eq:certified_coverage}
\end{equation}
A class contributes to this value only when at least one selected detector both reports enough historical source support and has a conservative calibration utility above the threshold; if $u_c>1$, no detector can satisfy that classwise-utility condition.

To express the same notion using the underlying population utilities rather than their calibration lower bounds, let $C_\mu^a(S;\boldsymbol u)$ replace the condition $a_{i,c}\ge u_c$ by $\mu_{i,c}^a\ge u_c$, where $a\in\{\mathrm{cal},\mathrm{dep}\}$ denotes calibration and deployment.
We consider a mixture-shift model in which each detector's mean Brier risk within a contributor--class cell remains stable while only the proportions of those cells change between calibration and deployment. This additional stability assumption is used only for the post-selection analysis in this subsection.
Let $R_{i,j,c}\in[0,1]$ be the mean Brier risk of detector $i$ in contributor--class cell $(j,c)$. We take $\pi_{j,c}^{\mathrm{cal}}=N^{-1}\sum_{t=1}^{N}\mathbf 1\{j_t=j,\,y_t=c\}$ as the record-weighted contributor--class composition of the calibration set, and let $\pi_{j,c}^{\mathrm{dep}}$ denote the corresponding deployment mixture.
For $a\in\{\mathrm{cal},\mathrm{dep}\}$, define
\begin{equation}
\pi_c^a=\sum_j\pi_{j,c}^a,
\qquad
\pi_{j\mid c}^a=\frac{\pi_{j,c}^a}{\pi_c^a},
\end{equation}
assuming $\pi_c^a>0$ for every class considered in the certificate.
We then define
\begin{equation}
R_i^a=\sum_{j,c}\pi_{j,c}^a R_{i,j,c},
\qquad
\mu_{i,c}^a=\sum_j\pi_{j\mid c}^a(1-R_{i,j,c}),
\label{eq:mixture_quantities}
\end{equation}
and let $\operatorname{TV}(p,q)=\tfrac12\|p-q\|_1$.
Under the cellwise mean-risk assumption, $R_i^{\mathrm{cal}}$ and $\mu_{i,c}^{\mathrm{cal}}$ coincide with the calibration quantities in Eqs.~\ref{eq:heterogeneous_overall_mean} and~\ref{eq:heterogeneous_class_mean}, respectively.

Because $1-R_{i,j,c}\in[0,1]$, a within-class bound
$\operatorname{TV}(\pi_{\cdot\mid c}^{\mathrm{dep}},
\pi_{\cdot\mid c}^{\mathrm{cal}})\le\eta_c$
implies $|\mu_{i,c}^{\mathrm{dep}}-\mu_{i,c}^{\mathrm{cal}}|\le\eta_c$.

\begin{theorem}[Certified coverage under mixture shift]
\label{thm:post_selection_certificates}
Let $\boldsymbol\vartheta\in[0,1]^C$ be prespecified and suppose that
$\operatorname{TV}(\pi_{\cdot\mid c}^{\mathrm{dep}},
\pi_{\cdot\mid c}^{\mathrm{cal}})\le\eta_c$
for every class $c$, where $\eta_c\in[0,1]$.
On the simultaneous calibration event, every adaptively selected set $\widehat S$ satisfies
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
C_\mu^{\mathrm{cal}}(\widehat S;
\boldsymbol\vartheta+\boldsymbol\eta+2\boldsymbol r)
&\le C_{\mathrm{cert}}(\widehat S;
\boldsymbol\vartheta+\boldsymbol\eta)\\
&\le C_\mu^{\mathrm{dep}}(\widehat S;\boldsymbol\vartheta)
\le C_{\mathrm{src}}(\widehat S),
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:certified_coverage_sandwich}
\end{equation}
where $(\boldsymbol r)_c=r_c$ and $(\boldsymbol\eta)_c=\eta_c$.
Moreover, if $\operatorname{TV}(\pi^{\mathrm{dep}},\pi^{\mathrm{cal}})\le\eta$ for some $\eta\in[0,1]$, then
\begin{equation}
R_i^{\mathrm{dep}}\le R_i^{\mathrm{cal}}+\eta.
\label{eq:risk_mixture_shift}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The simultaneous calibration result and the mixture-shift assumption give
\[
\mu_{i,c}^{\mathrm{cal}}\ge\vartheta_c+\eta_c+2r_c
\Longrightarrow a_{i,c}\ge\vartheta_c+\eta_c
\Longrightarrow\mu_{i,c}^{\mathrm{dep}}\ge\vartheta_c.
\]
Combining these implications with the fixed historical-support condition $\Gamma_{i,c}=1$, taking the existence condition over the selected detectors, and summing the class weights yields Eq.~\ref{eq:certified_coverage_sandwich}.
For the final result, total variation bounds the change in the expectation of any function taking values in $[0,1]$.
Applying this fact to the contributor--class risks gives Eq.~\ref{eq:risk_mixture_shift}.
\end{proof}

The theorem separates three quantities that serve different purposes.
$C_{\mathrm{src}}$ is the historical source-coverage value optimized during selection.
$C_{\mathrm{cert}}$ keeps only the part of that coverage that also has conservative calibration evidence.
$C_\mu^{\mathrm{dep}}$ describes the corresponding deployment utility after mixture shift.
The certificate quantifies the portion of the selected historical coverage that remains supported by classwise deployment utility under the stated mixture-shift bound.

% \section{Discussion and Conclusion}
% \label{sec:discussion_conclusion}
% In summary, \Fname enables selective reuse of pretrained intrusion detectors after raw historical source records are no longer accessible. Under a hard deployment budget, it first preserves maximum weighted source class coverage and then refines current predictive behavior before fitting a shared stacker. Across four tasks, a 50\% selection-cost budget reduces logical communication by 49.3\%--50.4\%, with the macro-F1 loss bounded by 0.028.

% \noindent\textbf{Coverage Priority and Validity.}
% The ordered objective is appropriate when source class preservation is a deployment requirement rather than a quantity that may be traded freely for average quality. RQ1 shows a case where utility-only selection sacrifices DDoS coverage, whereas the heterogeneity ablation shows cases where the constraint is naturally non-binding. Source coverage is nevertheless not a certificate of current accuracy: calibration and sealed testing remain necessary after distribution shift. The primary dynamic program is exact, while secondary refinement has only the stated one-swap certificate. The deployment-side mixture-shift certificate further assumes stable client--class conditional behavior and bounded mixture change; larger shifts require renewed validation.

% \noindent\textbf{Deployment and Privacy Limits.}
% Reported savings are logical bytes rather than measured latency, and exact selection scales exponentially with the attack-class count $C$. Optional protection applies fresh client-side noise and gives label-conditional LDP only to uploaded scores, not labels or detector parameters. At strong perturbation, attack leakage exceeds 0.5, so privacy must be balanced against security utility. Missing source records do not make the retained models private.
